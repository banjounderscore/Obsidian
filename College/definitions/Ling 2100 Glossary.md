# Phonetics

How speech sounds are physically produced and perceived (e.g. *bet* vs. *beet*).

---

# Phonology

The grammar of how sounds pattern and change by context (e.g. plural /s/ is [s] in *cats*, [z] in *dogs*).

---

# Morphology

The study of words and their parts: structure, meaning, and rules of realization in context.

---

# Syntax

The study of word order: which combinations of words are grammatical vs. ungrammatical.

---

# Semantics

The study of literal meaning and the inferences drawn from words and sentences.

---

# Pragmatics

The study of inferences drawn from utterances by people in context (meaning beyond what's said).

---

# Sociolinguistics

The study of the social meaning of language variation (e.g. what an accent leads listeners to infer about a speaker).

---

# Competence

Implicit, unobservable knowledge of your language (e.g. knowing "the the book" isn't allowed). Contrast: performance.

---

# Performance

What you actually say or do, including slips; observable. Contrast: competence.

---

# Prescriptive

*prescriptivism*

Rules about how people *should* talk ("Don't say *ain't*"). Subjective. Reporting that people hold such a norm is itself descriptive.

---

# Descriptive

*descriptivism*

Describing how people actually talk, without judging it. Objective; the linguist's stance.

---

# Arbitrariness

*arbitrary*

No natural link between a word's sound and its meaning (*whale*, *yell*). The opposite of iconicity.

---

# Iconicity

*iconic*

Form resembles meaning: onomatopoeia (*buzz*), bouba/kiki, reduplication ("more is more").

---

# Onomatopoeia

A word that imitates the sound it names (*buzz, boom*). A form of iconicity.

---

# Bouba/kiki effect

*bouba, kiki*

People reliably match round shapes with *bouba* and spiky shapes with *kiki*: evidence of sound symbolism.

---

# Frequency Code

Ohala: high pitch ~ small, low pitch ~ large. Explains why diminutive suffixes (*-ito, -y*) favor high vowels.

---

# Hockett's Design Features

*design features*

Features distinguishing human language from animal communication: displacement, productivity, arbitrariness, duality of patterning, prevarication, reflexiveness, etc. "Languages change over time" is NOT one.

---

# Displacement

Hockett feature: language can discuss absent, imaginary, or hypothetical things.

---

# Prevarication

Hockett feature: language can be used to lie.

---

# Duality of patterning

Hockett feature: meaningless sounds combine into meaningful units (morphemes/words), which combine again.

---

# Productivity

*productive*

The ability to create and understand infinitely many novel utterances; for a morphological process, how freely it applies to new words.

---

# Reflexiveness

Hockett feature: language can be used to talk about language.

---

# Ambiguity

*ambiguous*

The same words/sounds/morphemes in the same order with more than one meaning. Lexical (*bank*) or structural (*unlockable*, *American art teacher*).

---

# Lexical ambiguity

One word with multiple meanings (*bank*: river / financial; *Iraqi Head Seeks Arms*).

---

# Structural ambiguity

One string with two different structures/trees (*unlockable*; *Pope's Baby Steps on Gays*).

---

# Synchronic

Variation across speakers at one point in time (e.g. regional accents today).

---

# Diachronic

Change over time (e.g. the Great Vowel Shift).

---

# Morpheme

*morphemes*

The smallest piece of a word with a meaning or function; can't be divided further (*walk + -ing*).

---

# IPA

International Phonetic Alphabet: one symbol per sound, never ambiguous (unlike spelling).

---

# Homograph

*homographs*

Same spelling, different pronunciation/meaning (*close* the verb vs. *close* the adjective).

---

# Ingressive

Airflow into the mouth (clicks, implosives).

---

# Egressive

Airflow out of the mouth. Pulmonic egressive (lungs push air out) is the default for virtually all speech.

---

# Pulmonic

Airstream driven by the lungs; pulmonic egressive is the default for speech.

---

# Clicks

*click*

Velaric ingressive consonants: the tongue traps air between two closures and pulls it inward. Not in English.

---

# Implosives

*implosive*

Glottalic ingressive consonants: the larynx lowers during a closure, drawing air in. Not in English.

---

# Ejectives

*ejective*

Glottalic egressive consonants: the larynx rises to push air out through a closure; marked ʼ. Not in English.

---

# Place of articulation

Where in the vocal tract the constriction happens (bilabial, alveolar, velar...).

---

# Manner of articulation

How airflow is shaped (stop, fricative, nasal, approximant...).

---

# Voicing

*voiced, voiceless*

Whether the vocal folds vibrate. Voiceless English sounds: p t k f θ s ʃ h tʃ. Everything else is voiced.

---

# Bilabial

Both lips: p b m w.

---

# Labiodental

Lower lip + upper teeth: f v.

---

# Dental

Tongue against the upper teeth: θ ð.

---

# Alveolar

Tongue against the alveolar ridge: t d n s z l.

---

# Post-alveolar

*postalveolar*

Tongue just behind the alveolar ridge: ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ.

---

# Retroflex

Tongue tip curled back. Not in English.

---

# Palatal

Tongue body + hard palate: j.

---

# Velar

Tongue body + velum (soft palate): k g ŋ.

---

# Uvular

Back of tongue + uvula. Not in English.

---

# Pharyngeal

Tongue root + pharynx wall. Not in English.

---

# Glottal

At the vocal folds: h ʔ.

---

# Plosive

*plosives, stops*

Full closure, then release: p b t d k g ʔ.

---

# Fricative

*fricatives*

Narrow, turbulent airflow: f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h.

---

# Affricate

*affricates*

A stop released as a fricative in one segment: tʃ dʒ.

---

# Approximant

*approximants*

Articulators close but with no turbulence: ɹ j w; lateral approximant l.

---

# Trill

Articulator vibrates rapidly. Not in English.

---

# Flap

*tap, flapping*

One quick tap of the tongue: [ɾ] in *butter*. In English, /t/ flaps between vowels when not at a stressed onset.

---

# Liquid

*liquids*

ɹ and l.

---

# Glide

*glides*

w and j.

---

# Sonorant

*sonorants*

Made with a relatively open vocal tract, so air resonates freely: nasals, liquids, glides, vowels. Always voiced.

---

# Obstruent

*obstruents*

Made with a real obstruction of airflow: stops, fricatives, affricates. These contrast for voicing.

---

# Coarticulation

Neighboring sounds subtly affect each other's articulation.

---

# Palatography

Painting the tongue/palate to see where they touch: an empirical way to study articulation.

---

# McGurk effect

Mismatched audio and video change what sound you perceive: speech perception is multimodal.

---

# Schwa

[ə]: the most common vowel in unstressed syllables (the *a* in *sofa*).

---

# Diphthong

*diphthongs*

Two vowel qualities in one syllable: aɪ (*bite*), aʊ (*bout*), ɔɪ (*boy*).

---

# Monophthong

A single steady vowel.

---

# Lax vowel

*lax vowels, lax*

Shorter vowels (ɪ ʊ ɛ ɔ æ) that can't end an open syllable; they need a coda.

---

# Tense vowel

*tense vowels*

Longer vowels (i u e o) that can stand alone in an open syllable.

---

# Open syllable

A syllable ending in its vowel, with no coda (*bee*).

---

# Closed syllable

A syllable ending in a coda consonant (*cat*).

---

# Onset

Consonant(s) before the vowel in a syllable (/k/ in *cat*). Optional.

---

# Nucleus

The vowel of a syllable. The only required part.

---

# Coda

Consonant(s) after the vowel in a syllable (/t/ in *cat*).

---

# Chain shift

When one vowel moves, others follow to preserve contrasts (e.g. the Southern Vowel Shift).

---

# Merger

*mergers*

Two vowels become one for some speakers (*pin/pen*, *cot/caught*), often before a sonorant.

---

# Great Vowel Shift

The 1400s–1700s English vowel change that explains many spelling/pronunciation mismatches.

---

# Phoneme

*phonemes*

A distinct sound category in the mind, written /slashes/. Contrastive: swapping it changes meaning. Proven by a minimal pair.

---

# Allophone

*allophones*

A predictable variant of one phoneme, written [brackets]. In complementary distribution AND phonetically similar (e.g. [p]/[pʰ]).

---

# Minimal pair

*minimal pairs*

Two words differing in exactly one sound with different meanings (*sip/zip*): proves two different phonemes.

---

# Complementary distribution

Two sounds never occur in the same context; each appears only where the other can't. Necessary but not sufficient for allophones ([h]/[ŋ]).

---

# Contrastive

Swapping one sound for another in the same spot changes the meaning: the mark of separate phonemes.

---

# Free variation

Two sounds can occur in the same spot with no rule deciding which and no change in meaning.

---

# Aspiration

*aspirated*

A puff of air after a stop's release, [ʰ]. English /p t k/ are aspirated at the onset of a stressed syllable.

---

# Fortition

Strengthening of a sound (e.g. aspiration).

---

# Lenition

Weakening of a sound (e.g. flapping).

---

# Assimilation

A sound becomes more like a neighboring sound (nasal place assimilation *in-* → [ɪm]balance; voicing assimilation *-s* → [z] in *dogs*).

---

# Epenthesis

Inserting a sound to break up a hard sequence: [ə] in *foxes*, [n] in *an hour*.

---

# Natural class

A set of sounds sharing features, so one rule covers them all (aspiration applies to all of {p, t, k}).

---

# Allomorph

*allomorphs*

A variant pronunciation of one morpheme: plural [s]/[z]/[əz]; *a/an*; *in-* as [ɪn]/[ɪm]/[ɪŋ].

---

# Phonotactics

Which sound sequences a language allows (English /spr-/ ✓, /spm-/ ✗).

---

# Consonant cluster

*clusters*

Two or more consonants in a row with no vowel between (*str-* in *strengths*).

---

# Token

*tokens*

Counting every occurrence of a word, repeats included. "I came, I saw, I conquered" = 6 tokens (but 4 types).

---

# Stem

The base you add endings to (*walk* in *walked, walking*).

---

# Lemma

The dictionary form standing for a whole family of forms, even irregular ones (*go* covers *went, gone*).

---

# Romanization

Writing a language in the Latin alphabet when it doesn't natively use it.

---

# Agreement

A word changes form to match a feature (person/number/gender) of another word: *she walks / they walk*; French *la belle maison*.

---

# Narrow transcription

Phonetic transcription with maximal detail ([pʰɪn]).

---

# Broad transcription

Phonemic transcription, phonemes only (/pɪn/).

---

# Interlinear gloss

*gloss, glosses, glossed*

Three lines: original language → morpheme-by-morpheme translation (with labels like PST, PL, ASP) → free translation.

---

# WALS

World Atlas of Language Structures: a database of structural features across languages.

---

# SVO

Subject–Verb–Object word order, English's basic order (*I threw the ball*).

---

# Part of speech

*parts of speech, POS*

A word's category (noun, verb, adjective...). Determines where it goes and what morphology it takes; diagnosed by syntactic behavior.

---

# Determiner

*determiners*

Combines with a noun to pick out its referent: new/familiar (*a/the*), near/far (*this/that*), possession (*my*), quantity (*every, five*).

---

# Quantifier

*quantifiers*

A determiner expressing quantity: *every, some, all, five*.

---

# Copula

The linking verb *be* that joins a subject to a predicate (*the dress **is** red*).

---

# Modal auxiliary

*modal, modals, modal auxiliaries*

*can, may, would, should, might, will*: combines with a verb to describe hypothetical states of affairs.

---

# Argument

*arguments*

A participant noun a verb requires (*I* and *the ball* in *I threw the ball*). Not the debate sense.

---

# Intransitive

1 argument (subject only): *I swam*, *The window broke*.

---

# Transitive

2 arguments (subject + object): *I threw the ball*.

---

# Ditransitive

3 arguments (subject + two objects): *I gave her the book* (giver, recipient, thing given).

---

# Nominative

Subject case: *she, he, I*.

---

# Accusative

Object case: *her, him, me*.

---

# Genitive

Possessive case: *her, his, my*.

---

# Declension

Inflection of nouns/pronouns for case (*she → her*).

---

# Content word

*content words, open class*

Nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs: easy to paraphrase, new ones coined constantly.

---

# Function word

*function words, closed class*

Pronouns, determiners, conjunctions, prepositions, modals: hard to paraphrase, rarely new (but conjunction *slash*), most frequent.

---

# Inflection

*inflectional, inflected, inflect*

A word changes form for its grammatical context but stays the same part of speech (*dog→dogs*, *walk→walked*, *she→her*). Always outermost.

---

# Derivation

*derivational, derived*

Making a new word by changing a root's meaning or part of speech (*teach→teacher*, *popular→popularize*).

---

# Concatenative

Morphology by adding affixes (*cat→cats*). English plurals use a concatenative suffix.

---

# Non-concatenative

Morphology without adding affixes: ablaut or suppletion.

---

# Ablaut

Changing a vowel instead of adding an affix (*man/men*, *sing/sang*).

---

# Suppletion

*suppletive*

Replacing the whole root with an unrelated form (*good/better/best*, *be/am/was*).

---

# Free morpheme

*free morphemes*

A morpheme that can stand alone as a word (*car, tall, I*).

---

# Bound morpheme

*bound morphemes*

A morpheme that can't stand alone (*-ing, un-, -s, -ize*).

---

# Cranberry morpheme

*cranberry morphemes*

A bound morpheme occurring in only one word, with no independent meaning (*cran-*, *rasp-*).

---

# Root

*roots*

The core morpheme of a word, usually free (*nation* in *nationalizes*). In dependency grammar, the ROOT is the word nothing depends on (the main verb).

---

# Affix

*affixes, AFF*

A bound morpheme attached to a root or stem: prefix, suffix, circumfix, infix.

---

# Prefix

*prefixes*

An affix before the root (***re**pay*).

---

# Suffix

*suffixes*

An affix after the root (*pay**ing***).

---

# Circumfix

An affix on both sides of the root at once (***en**light**en***).

---

# Infix

An affix inserted inside the root. English only has *abso-effing-lutely*.

---

# Neologism

*neologisms*

A newly coined word (*monoamorous, rizz*).

---

# Blend

*blends, blending*

Fusing parts of two words: *brunch, smog, motel, Brexit*.

---

# Acronym

*acronyms*

A word from initial letters: *NASA, radar, BFF*.

---

# Clipping

*clipped*

Shortening one word: *gym, math, mic, rizz* (charisma).

---

# Borrowing

*borrowings, loanword, loanwords*

Taking a word from another language (*karaoke, sauna, algebra*).

---

# Back-formation

Making a "base" by removing something that looks like an affix: *enthuse* from *enthusiasm*.

---

# Genericization

*genericized*

A brand name becomes a general word: *Kleenex, Xerox, Uber*.

---

# Compound

*compounds, compounding*

Two whole words combined: *desk chair, sunglasses, brainrot*. The relation between the parts is unspecified.

---

# Reduplication

Repeating a root to change meaning (*salad salad*). Iconic: "more is more."

---

# Corpus

*corpora*

A large body of real text used for analysis (Brown Corpus, Reddit comments).

---

# Zipf's Frequency Law

*Zipf's law, frequency law*

A few words are very frequent (function words); very many are rare (content words). A power law.

---

# Zipf's Brevity Law

*brevity law*

More frequent words are shorter. Evidence that language is efficient.

---

# Power law

*power-law*

A long-tail distribution (frequency ∝ ~1/rank), unlike a bell curve.

---

# Isolating

About 1 morpheme per word, little inflection (Yoruba, Mandarin).

---

# Agglutinating

A root plus a string of affixes, each with one meaning (Turkish *ev-imiz-de* "in our house").

---

# Polysynthetic

Very long words with many bound morphemes; a whole sentence can be one word (Yupik).

---

# Fusional

One affix fuses several meanings (French *était*: be + 3rd person + singular + imperfective).

---

# Analytic

Grammatical info carried mostly by separate words, not affixes. English is between isolating and agglutinating.

---

# Artifact

*artifacts*

Something made by humans for a purpose; defined by function (*stapler*). *Finish a sandwich* = finish making/eating it.

---

# Natural kind

*natural kinds*

Something that exists independently of humans; defined by essence (*bear, spinach*).

---

# Grammatical

*grammaticality*

Allowed by the language's syntax. Ungrammatical strings are marked with \*.

---

# Ungrammatical

Not allowed by the syntax (\**Student the read book the*). Can still be meaningful (*Me love dog*).

---

# Do-support

Inserting *do/did* when there's no other auxiliary: questions (*Did she read it?*) and negation (*She did not read it*).

---

# Subject–auxiliary inversion

*inversion*

The auxiliary moves before the subject in questions (*Will the student read it?*).

---

# Polar question

*yes/no question, polar questions*

A yes/no question. English uses do-support and inversion; Mandarin adds *ma*; French adds *est-ce que*.

---

# Wh-question

*wh-questions, content question*

A question with a *who/what/which* word. In English it's fronted, leaving a gap; Mandarin leaves it in place.

---

# Relative clause

*relative clauses*

A clause modifying a noun: *the book [that the student read ___]*.

---

# Long-distance dependency

*long-distance dependencies, non-local dependency*

Two words that belong together but are separated in the string (*What did you eat ___?*; *The key to the cabinets is...*).

---

# Constituent

*constituents, constituency*

A group of words acting as a unit; a subtree in the tree. Shown by constituency tests.

---

# Constituency test

*constituency tests*

YES to any → a constituent: (1) can answer a question, (2) sticks together when reordered, (3) can be conjoined.

---

# Lexical category

*lexical categories*

Word-level categories (Det, N, Adj, TransV, IntransV, Prep) that determine a word's distribution.

---

# Phrasal category

*phrasal categories*

Phrase-level categories (NP, VP, AdjP) that determine a phrase's distribution.

---

# Noun Phrase

*NP, noun phrases*

A phrase built around a noun: *every book, the red ball, two people*.

---

# Verb Phrase

*VP, verb phrases*

A phrase built around a verb, possibly containing an NP: *ran, threw the ball*.

---

# Phrase Structure Grammar

*phrase structure, PS rules, phrase structure rules, phrase structure rule*

Chomsky's rule system (S → NP VP...) that captures hierarchy, word order, and grammaticality. Generative, recursive, one-to-many, language-specific.

---

# Terminal node

*terminal, terminal nodes*

The bottom of a tree: actual words, supplied by the lexicon.

---

# Non-terminal node

*non-terminal*

A category label (S, NP, VP) expanded by phrase structure rules.

---

# Lexicon

The list of words and their categories (Det → *the, a*...).

---

# Generative

Rules that produce all (and only) the grammatical sentences, including new ones.

---

# Recursive

*recursion*

A rule can invoke itself (NP → NP PP; PP → P NP), so there is no longest sentence.

---

# One-to-many

One left-hand side with several right-hand expansions (VP → V NP and VP → V).

---

# Transformation

*transformations, movement*

An extra rule capturing long-distance dependencies, e.g. a wh-word "moving" to the front (a metaphor, not literal).

---

# Dependency grammar

*dependency, dependencies, dependency parsing*

Tesnière's framework: binary, asymmetric, typed head→dependent relations between POS-tagged words. Works for any word order; standard in NLP.

---

# Dependency length

How many words a dependency arc spans. Corpora favor short ones (37 languages): language is efficient.

---

# Universal Dependencies

Stanford's consistent dependency annotation scheme, with treebanks in 100+ languages.

---

# NLP

*natural language processing*

Computational processing of language. Uses POS tagging and dependency parsing.

---

# POS tagging

*POS-tagged*

Automatically labeling each word's part of speech (DT, NN, VBD...).

---

# Truth conditions

What the world must be like for a sentence to be true.

---

# Entailment

*entails, entailments*

If A is true, B must be true (*I ran* → *I exercised*). Can't be cancelled; disappears under negation.

---

# Contradiction

*contradicts*

Two sentences that can't both be true (*Alice has a cat* / *Alice has no pets*).

---

# Lexical semantics

The meaning of open-class content words, and how noun/verb/adjective types differ.

---

# Compositional semantics

How word meanings (especially function words) combine into sentence meanings.

---

# Synonym

*synonyms*

Same meaning: *couch/sofa*.

---

# Antonym

*antonyms*

Opposite meaning: *tall/short*. Mostly adjectives (gradable scales).

---

# Unmarked

The neutral member of an antonym pair that names the whole scale: *How tall is she?* (vs. marked *How short is she?*).

---

# Homonymy

*homonym, homonyms*

Unrelated meanings that accidentally share a form (*bank/bank*, *for/four*). Not expected to recur across languages.

---

# Polysemy

*polysemous*

Related meanings of one word (*head* of body / of school; *lamb* animal / meat). Potentially systematic; may recur across languages.

---

# Light verb

*light verbs*

A semantically weak verb plus a noun carrying the meaning (Persian "do a run"; English *take a bath*).

---

# Family resemblance

*family resemblances*

Wittgenstein: category members share overlapping similarities, not one common feature (*game*). Words lack strict boundaries.

---

# Relational noun

*relational*

A noun that needs a relatum: *sister* (someone's sister). Contrast: sortal.

---

# Sortal noun

*sortal*

A noun that just names a kind: *sheep*.

---

# Mass noun

*mass nouns, mass*

Stuff, not counted: *water, mail, furniture* (*so much furniture*, \**two furnitures*).

---

# Count noun

*count nouns*

Countable units: *books, letters, apples*.

---

# Stative

A verb describing a state, with no change (*Alice loved movies*). Contrast: dynamic.

---

# Agentive

The subject acts deliberately (*Alice ran*). Non-agentive: *Alice fell*.

---

# Telic

Has a built-in endpoint; takes "in X time" (*ate the pizza in 10 minutes*).

---

# Atelic

No natural endpoint; takes "for X time" (*ate pizza for 10 minutes*, *watched TV*).

---

# Argument structure

How many arguments a verb takes and what roles they play (*The submarine sank the ship* / *The ship sank*).

---

# Causative

A verb entailing its object changes state (*broke*: → *The fence broke*). Non-causative: *hit*.

---

# Gradable

*gradable adjectives*

Allows degrees: *very tall, taller*. Non-gradable: *?very dead*.

---

# Vagueness

What counts as *tall/expensive* depends on the comparison class (tall man vs. tall building).

---

# Faultless disagreement

Two people disagree and neither is wrong: the mark of subjective predicates (*Roller coasters are fun*).

---

# Intersective

*intersective adjective*

Adj N = Adj things ∩ N things: *Swedish musician*, *American*.

---

# Subsective

*subsective adjective*

Adj N is a subset of N but not just "Adj things": a *skillful musician* isn't necessarily a skillful chef.

---

# Privative

*privative adjective*

Adj N is NOT an N: *former musician, fake gun*.

---

# Deixis

*deictic*

"Pointing" words whose referent depends on context (who/when/where): *I, you, here, now, today, tomorrow*.

---

# Expressive

*expressives*

Adds speaker attitude without changing truth conditions: *my **damn** keys*.

---

# Slur

*slurs*

Same descriptive content as a neutral term plus a derogatory attitude toward a group.

---

# Conceptual metaphor

*metaphor, metaphors*

Understanding the abstract via the concrete (TIME IS MONEY, GOOD IS UP). Lakoff & Johnson: fundamental to thought, not decoration. No "like/as."

---

# Simile

A comparison using "like" or "as."

---

# Allegory

An extended story whose parts stand for abstract ideas.

---

# Lambda calculus

*typed lambda calculus*

A framework treating word meanings as functions over types *e* (individuals) and *t* (truth values): *swim* = λx[swim(x)].

---

# Referent

*referents*

The thing in the world a word or phrase picks out.

---

# Bridging inference

*bridging*

Inferring that *the* refers to something related to a mentioned entity: *I was in a car. The seat was comfortable.*

---

# Speech act

*speech acts*

An utterance that does something. Every assertion proposes a Common Ground update.

---

# Performative

*performatives*

Austin: an utterance that makes itself true when said by the right person in the right context (*I hereby pronounce you...*).

---

# Common Ground

The propositions all participants mutually (know they) take for granted (Grice, Stalnaker).

---

# Theory of Mind

Your model of what's in other people's minds and how it differs from yours.

---

# Assertion

*assertions*

A proposal to add a proposition to the Common Ground. It can be accepted or rejected.

---

# Presupposition

*presuppositions, presupposes*

Info assumed to already be in the Common Ground (*The King of France is bald* → there is one). If false, the sentence is nonsensical. Projects through negation.

---

# Projection

*project*

A presupposition survives under negation, modals, and questions (*The King is NOT bald* → still a King).

---

# Factive verb

*factive, factive verbs*

A verb presupposing its complement is true: *know, realize, regret* (*Sam knows it's raining* → it's raining).

---

# Grice's Maxims

*maxims, maxim, Cooperative Principle*

Quality (be truthful), Quantity (as informative as needed), Manner (be clear), Relevance (be relevant). Politeness is NOT a maxim.

---

# Quality

*Maxim of Quality*

Say only what you believe true and have evidence for.

---

# Quantity

*Maxim of Quantity*

Be as informative as required, no more and no less.

---

# Manner

*Maxim of Manner*

Be clear, organized, and understandable.

---

# Relevance

*Maxim of Relevance, Relation*

Be relevant to the current purpose of the conversation.

---

# Conversational implicature

*implicature, implicatures, implicate, implicated*

An inference beyond what's said, from assuming speakers follow the maxims. Context-dependent, cancelable, calculable, reinforceable.

---

# Scalar implicature

Asserting a weaker claim implies the stronger one is false (*some* → not all).

---

# Cancelable

*cancellable, cancelled, cancel*

Can be retracted without contradiction (*Some passed, in fact all did*). True of implicatures, not entailments or presuppositions.

---

# Calculable

An implicature can be worked out by reasoning from what was said plus the maxims.

---

# Reinforceable

An implicature can be stated outright without sounding redundant (*some, but not all*).

---

# Plausible deniability

Because implicatures are cancelable, speakers can deny meaning them: more polite and diplomatic.

---

# Question Under Discussion

*QUD, QUDs*

Roberts 2012: the implicit question a conversation is addressing. It defines what's relevant and informative enough.

---

# Adjacency pair

*adjacency pairs*

A conventional pairing of discourse moves: greeting/greeting, question/answer, invitation/acceptance or refusal.

---

# Dispreferred

A reply that goes against the expected one (e.g. refusing an invitation), often softened or marked with *well*.
