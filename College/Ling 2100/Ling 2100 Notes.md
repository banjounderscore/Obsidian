# Unit 1 — Introduction to Linguistics

## Why study language
- A defining feature of humanity — substance of social interaction, law, literature.
- **Productive/generative**: infinite new sentences from finite rules, not memorized.
- Applications: NLP, speech pathology, forensics, translation, language teaching.

## Facts
- ~**6,000 languages** spoken today; 90% of people speak only 10% of them.
- **WALS** (World Atlas of Language Structures) = database of structural properties across languages.
- **Endangered Languages Project** = tracks language vitality.
- **Word order**: languages are classified by the order of Subject/Verb/Object.
  - **SVO** = Subject-Verb-Object — English (*I threw the ball*).
  - **OVS** = object first, subject last — some languages work this way.
- Turkish has a verb tense used just for gossip; some languages don't mark verbs for **past tense** at all.

## Themes
1. Language is **systematic** — studied scientifically.
2. Systematic rules → **infinite ideas from finite means**.
3. Language **varies systematically** (not randomly).
4. Diverse across languages, but shares **universals**.
5. Much of language is **arbitrary**.
6. Grammar = complex, **largely implicit** rules.
7. Children **acquire** language without explicit teaching — innate.
8. All languages **change over time** (a theme, NOT a Hockett feature — see below).

> ⚠️ Since language is universal but explicit teaching isn't (tied to specific cultures/schooling), language *can't* depend on being taught.

## Competence vs. Performance
| | Competence | Performance |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Implicit knowledge of your language | What you actually say/do |
| Observable? | No | Yes |
| Example | No rule allows "the, the, the book" | You might still say it (a slip) |

## Speech Communication Chain
Think of what to say → choose words → order them → recall pronunciation → articulate → *(hearer)* perceive sounds → map to words/sentence → understand → infer speaker's meaning/intent.
- Only **~0.3 sec** gap between conversational turns — next utterance is planned before the other person finishes.

## Subfields
| Subfield | Studies | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Phonetics | Physical sound production/perception | *bet* vs. *beet* |
| Phonology | Sound patterning & context-rules | /s/ → [s] in *cats*, [z] in *dogs* |
| Morphology | Word formation | plural of *child*? |
| Syntax | Word order/sentence structure | *I picked up the package* |
| Semantics | Literal meaning | what does *pick up* mean? |
| Pragmatics | Meaning in context | an indirect "no" |
| Sociolinguistics | Social meaning of variation | accent → inferences about speaker |

## Ambiguity
- **Lexical** (homophony): one sound, 2+ words — [kɚnəl] = *kernel*/*colonel*.
- **Structural**: same words, 2+ parses — "river bank" vs. "financial bank."
- ⚠️ Not the same as synchronic/diachronic variation, descriptive/prescriptive, or allophone variation — those aren't about multiple *meanings*.

## Language ≠ Writing
- Spoken language is ephemeral, ~50k–200k years old; writing is only **~6,000 years old** (Mesopotamian bookkeeping).
- Some languages have no writing system at all.
- Everyone acquires spoken language (like walking); not everyone learns to write (a taught skill, like ballet).
- **Writing records language; writing is not language.**

## Prescriptive vs. Descriptive
| Prescriptive (subjective)                  | Descriptive (objective)                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Don't say *ain't*."                       | "Some speakers use *ain't* to signal alienation from institutions."                       |
| "Don't end a sentence with a preposition." | "English adjectives precede nouns; English uses **SVO** word order (*I threw the ball*)." |

- **Grammar** (to a linguist) = implicit knowledge of your language, not school rules.
- **Descriptive** (as a stance): no way of speaking is objectively "better"; prescriptive judgments often mask stereotypes about *speakers*, not real deficits in the language.
- ⚠️ *Reporting on* a prescriptive attitude ("many people think X is nonstandard") is itself **descriptive** — reporting a norm ≠ enforcing one.

## Hockett's Design Features
*(what distinguishes human language from animal communication)*

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vocal-auditory / gestural-visual | Spoken & heard, or signed & seen |
| Intentionality | Speakers mean to say what they say |
| Transitoriness | Ephemeral unless recorded |
| Interchangeability | Anything hearable is also sayable |
| Total feedback | You perceive your own speech/signing |
| Specialization | The signal exists *to* communicate |
| Semanticity | Specific forms map to specific meanings |
| Arbitrariness | Sound–meaning link is convention |
| Discreteness | Continuous sound mapped to discrete category |
| Displacement | Can discuss absent/imaginary/hypothetical things |
| Prevarication | Can be used to lie |
| Duality of patterning | Sounds → morphemes → words (two combinable levels) |
| Productivity | Infinite novel utterances |
| Learnability | Can learn a new language |
| Cultural transmission | You learn the language(s) around you |
| Reflexiveness | Language can describe itself |

> ⚠️ "Languages change over time" is **NOT** on this list — it's a theme, not a Hockett feature.

- **Morpheme**: the smallest piece of a word that carries meaning on its own — you can't break it down any further without losing the meaning. *Walk* is one morpheme. *Walking* is two: *walk* + *-ing*. *Un-* and *-ing* are morphemes too, even though they can't stand alone as words.

## Arbitrariness vs. Iconicity
- **Iconic** = sound depicts meaning (*buzz, boom* = onomatopoeia).
- **Arbitrary** = no such link (*whale, kowtow, yell*).
- **Bouba/kiki effect**: nonsense words reliably matched to shapes (round = *bouba*, spiky = *kiki*).
- **Tongue Pull Hypothesis**: high tongue position → higher pitch (pulls larynx up).
- **Frequency Code** (Ohala): high pitch ~ small; low pitch ~ large → explains why **diminutive suffixes** (*-ito, -y*) favor **high vowels** (Jurafsky).
- Trade-off: arbitrary = more expressive power; iconic = easier to learn/remember.

# Unit 3.1 — Phonetics: Consonants

## Vocal tract
Lungs → **larynx** (vocal folds = voicing) → oral/nasal cavity, shaped by articulators (lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, palate, velum, uvula, tongue, glottis).

![[Ling 2100 Notes-1790220720526.webp|453]]

## Airstream mechanisms
- **Ingressive** = air flows *into* the mouth.
- **Egressive** = air flows *out* of the mouth.
- **Pulmonic egressive** = the default for virtually all speech: lungs push air out.
- **Non-pulmonic** (none used in English):
  - **Clicks** — velaric *ingressive* (tongue traps air between two closures and pulls it inward).
  - **Implosives** — glottalic *ingressive* (larynx lowers while a closure is held, drawing air in).
  - **Ejectives** — glottalic *egressive* (larynx raises to push air out through a closure; marked **ʼ**).

## Describing a consonant: Place / Manner / Voicing

**Place** (where the constriction happens):

| Place         | Definition                        | English examples |
| ------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Bilabial      | Both lips                         | p b m w          |
| Labiodental   | Lower lip + upper teeth           | f v              |
| Dental        | Tongue against upper teeth        | θ ð              |
| Alveolar      | Tongue against alveolar ridge     | t d n s z l      |
| Post-alveolar | Tongue just behind alveolar ridge | ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ        |
| Retroflex     | Tongue tip curled back            | *not in English* |
| Palatal       | Tongue body + hard palate         | j                |
| Velar         | Tongue body + velum (soft palate) | k g ŋ            |
| Uvular        | Tongue back + uvula               | *not in English* |
| Pharyngeal    | Tongue root + pharynx wall        | *not in English* |
| Glottal       | Vocal folds                       | h ʔ              |

**Manner** (how the airflow is shaped):

| Manner              | Definition                           | Example           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------- |
| Plosive/Stop        | Full closure, then release           | p b t d k g ʔ     |
| Nasal               | Oral closure, air through nose       | m n ŋ             |
| Trill               | Articulator vibrates rapidly         | *not in English*  |
| Tap/Flap            | One quick tap                        | ɾ (*butter*)      |
| Fricative           | Narrow, turbulent passage            | f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h |
| Lateral fricative   | Turbulent air around sides of tongue | *not in English*  |
| Approximant         | Close but no turbulence              | ɹ j (w)           |
| Lateral approximant | Air flows around sides of tongue     | l                 |

- **Liquid** = ɹ + l.
- **Glide** = w, j.
- **Affricate** = a stop released as a fricative in one segment (tʃ, dʒ).
- **Sonorants**: made with a relatively open vocal tract — air resonates freely, no major obstruction (nasals, liquids, glides, vowels — always voiced). 
- **Obstruents**: made with a real obstruction/constriction of airflow (stops, fricatives, affricates — these contrast for voicing).

**Voicing**: whether the vocal folds vibrate while the sound is made. 
- **Voiced** = vibrate {everything else} 
- **Voiceless** = don't vibrate {p t k f θ s ʃ h tʃ}

## IPA vs. English spelling
- IPA = 1 symbol per sound; spelling isn't (*sing* = 4 letters/3 sounds; *text* = 4 letters/**5 sounds**).
- Spelling is ambiguous (*Leslie* = [lɛsli] or [lɛzli]); IPA never is.
- ⚠️ Judge a word's final *sound*, not its final letter (*Emma* and *Hannah* both end in [ə]).
- **Homograph**: words spelled the same but pronounced/meaning differently (e.g. *close* the verb vs. *close* the adjective).
- **Pros of all-IPA spelling**: easier phonics; instant pronunciation; disambiguates homographs.
- **Cons**: loses etymology info carried by silent letters; inconsistent records across accents; one accent's IPA misrepresents another's.

## Coarticulation & variation
- **Coarticulation**: neighboring sounds subtly affect each other (e.g. [d] in "bad guy" vs. "bad person").
- **Palatography**: paints the tongue in charcoal to see where it touches the palate — studies articulation empirically.
- /ɹ/ varies by speaker: "molar/bunched" vs. "apical."

## Typology
- English's [θ, ð] (dental fricatives) are **typologically rare** cross-linguistically (Mielke 2017).
- More typologically common sounds are **acquired earlier** by children (stops before fricatives).
- **McGurk effect**: mismatched audio/visual input → sound is misperceived (e.g. seeing [v] but hearing [b]) — speech perception is multi-modal.

## Preview of phonology
- **Minimal pair** = words differing by exactly one sound → proves **different phonemes**.
  - *sip/zip* → /s/≠/z/; *math/mass* → /θ/≠/s/ — English-specific (not true in Spanish).

# Unit 3.2 — Phonetics: Vowels

## Vowels vs. consonants
- Airflow unrestricted; inherently voiced.
- Every vowel is really just a tongue position, described along 3 dimensions:
  - **Height**: how high the tongue is raised toward the roof of the mouth — high/mid/low. (Say "beet" [i]: tongue high. Say "bot" [ɑ]: tongue drops low.)
  - **Backness**: how far forward or back the tongue is in the mouth — front/central/back. (Say "beet" [i]: tongue bunched forward. Say "boot" [u]: tongue pulled back.)
  - **Rounding**: whether the lips are rounded/pursed or spread/neutral while making the sound.

## IPA vowel chart
Same row order (Height) and column order (Backness) as the definitions above. Each cell = unrounded, rounded. **Bold = used in English.**

| Height \ Backness | Front | Central | Back |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | **i, ɪ**, y | ɨ, ʉ | ɯ, **u, ʊ** |
| Mid | **e/eɪ, ɛ**, ø | **ə, ɚ, ʌ**, ɵ | ɤ, **o/oʊ, ɔ** |
| Low | **æ**, ɶ | ɐ | **ɑ**, ɒ |

Diphthongs: **aɪ** (bite), **aʊ** (bout), **ɔɪ** (boy).
- **Stress**: the syllable pronounced with extra emphasis — louder, longer, and/or higher pitch than its neighbors (e.g. *PENcil*, not *penCIL*).
- **Schwa [ə]** = most common vowel in **unstressed** syllables (e.g. the *a* in *sofa* [ˈsofə]).
- **Diphthong** = 2 vowel sounds in 1 syllable (vs. monophthong = 1 steady vowel).

## Tense vs. Lax
|          | Tense                           | Lax                            |
| -------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Vowels   | i, u, e/eɪ, o                   | ɪ, ʊ, ɛ, ɔ, æ                  |
| Duration | Longer                          | Shorter                        |
| Syllable | Can stand alone (open syllable) | Needs a coda (closed syllable) |

- **Open syllable**: ends in the vowel, nothing after it (e.g. *bee* [bi]).
- **Closed syllable**: ends in a coda (e.g. *cat* [kæt]).
- **Coda**: one or more consonants closing out a syllable after the vowel (e.g. in *cat*, /t/ is the coda).
- ⚠️ **Rule**: lax vowels can't stand alone in an open syllable — "boo" [bu] ✓, "buh" [bʊ] ✗. So **[kʊ]** is illegal; **[kæk]**/**[kæθ]** are fine (closed syllables).

## Vowels & sociolinguistic variation
- Vowels are continuous, not discrete, so they can shift gradually as a group.
- **Chain shift**: when one vowel moves position, others follow, to preserve the contrasts between them (e.g. the Southern Vowel Shift, below).
- **Synchronic** variation: across speakers, right now (e.g. different regional accents today).
- **Diachronic** variation: change over time (e.g. the Great Vowel Shift).

### Mergers
Often before a sonorant: Marry/merry/Mary, Pin/pen (South), Feel/fill (Appalachian), Cot/caught (young Americans).

### Shifts
- *(DRESS, FACE, PRIZE, etc. are standard vowel-class labels — each names the whole class of vowels found in that keyword, e.g. DRESS = the vowel in "dress," "bed," "head.")*
- **Southern Vowel Shift**: DRESS raises/fronts, FACE lowers/backs, PRIZE monophthongizes (fading).
- **Northern Cities Shift**: Great Lakes region (also fading).
- **Great Vowel Shift** (1400s–1700s): explains English spelling/pronunciation mismatches.
- Evidence without recordings comes from two places:
  - **Historical spellings**: e.g. *meat* ≠ *meet* in spelling, because they used to be pronounced differently.
  - **Rhymes that no longer rhyme**: e.g. in Shakespeare's poetry.

# Unit 4 — Phonology

## Phonetics vs. Phonology
- **Phonetics** = how sounds are made/perceived.
- **Phonology** = the grammar of how sounds pattern & vary by context.

## Phoneme vs. Allophone
Same two categories, shown two ways — **what it is**, and **how you prove it**:

| | Phoneme | Allophone |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A distinct sound category in the mind | A predictable variant of *one* phoneme |
| Written as | /slashes/ | [brackets] |
| Distribution | **Contrastive** — swapping it for another sound in the *same* spot changes the meaning | **Complementary** — the two sounds' contexts *complete* each other like puzzle pieces: each one only shows up where the other one can't, so between them they cover every case with no overlap |
| Proven by | A **minimal pair**: 2 words, 1 sound different, different meaning | No minimal pair exists, *and* the sounds are phonetically similar |
| Example | *sip*/*zip* → /s/ and /z/ are different phonemes | [p] and [pʰ] are both just /p/ (no minimal pair; both voiceless bilabial stops) |

> ⚠️ Complementary distribution alone isn't enough to prove two sounds are allophones — they also need to be **phonetically similar** (see [h]/[ŋ] below).
> ⚠️ A minimal pair is always 2 different **phonemes** — never 2 allophones, since allophones can't change meaning.

**Free variation**: a third possibility, different from both of the above — two sounds that could *both* show up in the exact same spot, with no rule deciding which, and no change in meaning (e.g. some speakers say either [i] or [aɪ] at the end of "the," with nothing predicting which). Neither contrastive (no meaning change) nor complementary (contexts overlap, no rule).

## Formalizing a rule
$$/A/ \rightarrow [B] \; / \; X\_\_Y$$ — "/A/ becomes [B] after X, before Y."
- Template: /X/ has allophones [X] and [Y]. [Y] occurs in one specific, describable context; [X] occurs **elsewhere** (whichever allophone shows up in the widest range of contexts is the "elsewhere" case).
- **Korean example**: [s] and [ʃ] are allophones of one phoneme /s/. [ʃ] only shows up before [i]; [s] shows up everywhere else → /s/ → [ʃ] / __[i]; [s] elsewhere.

## Syllable structure
Every syllable = (Onset) – Nucleus – (Coda); only the Nucleus is required.
- **Onset**: consonant(s) before the vowel (e.g. /k/ in *cat*).
- **Nucleus**: the vowel itself — required (e.g. /æ/ in *cat*).
- **Coda**: consonant(s) after the vowel (e.g. /t/ in *cat*).
- **C**: consonant (shorthand).
- **V**: vowel (shorthand) — together, e.g. *cat* = CVC.
- **Consonant cluster**: 2+ consonants in a row with no vowel between them. English allows fairly complex ones in both onset and coda — *strengths* is about as complex as it gets.

## Key English Rules
**1. Aspiration** = a brief puff of air released right after a stop's closure opens, marked [ʰ]. 
Data: [spɑt] *spot* vs. [pʰɑt] *pot*; [spæt] *spat* vs. [pʰæt] *pat*. No minimal pairs for [p]/[pʰ] → allophones of /p/ (same logic for /t/, /k/).
- **Rule**: /p, t, k/ → [pʰ, tʰ, kʰ] at the onset of a **stressed syllable**; [p, t, k] elsewhere (e.g. after /s/, or in an unstressed onset).
- Language-specific: **contrastive** in Thai (minimal pairs exist there), only allophonic in English.

**2. Flapping** = a stop (here, /t/) pronounced as a quick tap of the tongue against the alveolar ridge, [ɾ], instead of a full stop. 
Data: [æɾəm] *atom*, [əˈtʰɑmɪk] *atomic*, [ˈleɪɾɚ] *later*, [leɪt]/[leɪʔ] *late*. /t/ has (at least) 4 allophones:
- **[tʰ]**: at a stressed syllable's onset.
- **[ɾ]**: between vowels, when not at a stressed onset.
- **[ʔ]**: word-finally (some speakers).
- **[t]**: elsewhere.
- **Fortition** = strengthening (aspirating).
- **Lenition** = weakening (flapping — less distinctive from its voiced surroundings).
- **Assimilation** = the flap voices to match the surrounding (voiced) vowels.

**3. L-velarization ("dark L")** = when /l/ is pronounced with the back of the tongue raised toward the velum, producing a "dark" [ɫ] instead of a "clear" [l]. ⚠️ Not in slides, but on the practice exam. 
Data: "leaf" [lif] (onset) vs. "feel" [fiɫ] (coda); "light" [laɪt] (onset) vs. "tile" [taɪəɫ] (coda). [l] and [ɫ] are allophones of /l/.
- **Rule**: /l/ → [ɫ] in **coda** position; [l] elsewhere (onset).

**4. Nasal place assimilation** = a nasal consonant shifts its place of articulation to match the consonant right after it. 
Data: [ɪntʰɑləɹənt] *intolerant*, [ɪmbɑləns] *imbalance*, [ɪŋkʰəɹɛkt] *incorrect*. The prefix "in-" surfaces as [ɪn]/[ɪm]/[ɪŋ] depending on what follows.
- **Rule**: a nasal **assimilates to the place of articulation** of the following consonant.

**5. Voicing assimilation** = a suffix consonant shifts its voicing to match the sound right before it. 
Data: [pleɪd] *played* vs. [wɑlkt] *walked*; [dɔgz] *dogs* vs. [kʰæts] *cats*.
- **Rule**: the past-tense/plural suffix matches the voicing of the preceding sound (voiced→[d]/[z], voiceless→[t]/[s]).
- **Epenthesis**: inserting an extra sound to break up a sequence that's hard/impossible to pronounce — [ə] is epenthesized when the stem already ends in a sound too similar to the suffix (*foxes* [fɑksəz]).

> ⚠️ "Singer" = **[sɪŋɚ]** — no [g] (contrast "finger" [fɪŋgɚ], same morpheme). Plural "singers" = **[sɪŋɚz]** — voicing assimilates to [z] after the voiced ɚ.

### [h]/[ŋ]: complementary distribution ≠ allophones
[h] (syllable-initial) and [ŋ] (coda) are in complementary distribution but **share no distinctive features** → most phonologists say NOT allophones.

## Natural classes & features
**Natural class** = sounds sharing features, so one rule covers the whole class (aspiration applies to all of {p, t, k}, not just /p/).
Features:
- **[±voice]**: vocal folds vibrating or not.
- **[±nasal]**: air escapes through the nose or not.
- **[±continuant]**: airflow continuous (fricatives/vowels) vs. blocked (stops).
- **[±sonorant]**: resonant/voiced (vowels, nasals, liquids, glides) vs. obstructed (stops, fricatives, affricates).
- Plus place features: labial/coronal/dorsal.

## Allomorphs
Like allophones, but tied to a specific **morpheme** (need not be in complementary distribution; must be semantically equivalent). E.g. the plural morpheme "-s" is pronounced [s] in *cats*, [z] in *dogs*, and [əz] in *foxes* — same suffix, same meaning, different pronunciation depending on context. Same idea with "a" vs. "an": same word, [ə haʊs] *a house* vs. [ən aʊɚ] *an hour*.
- "a/an": [ə]/[ən] — **epenthesis** of [n] before a vowel.
- "in-": [m]/[n]/[ŋ] — nasal place assimilation.
- Past tense "-ed": [t]/[d]/[əd]. Plural "-s": [s]/[z]/[əz]. — both voicing assimilation + epenthesis.

## Phonotactics
**Phonotactics** = which sound sequences are legal in a language (English: /spr-/ ✓, /spm-/ ✗).
- Cross-linguistic complexity: Simple (CV only) → Moderate (≤CCVC) → Complex (>CCVC).
- English, by word **type** (Yang 2016, CMU dict): commonest consonants /n l s t/; rarest /ʒ ð θ/ — though /ð/ is common by **token** (via "the"). Commonest vowels /ʌ ə ɪ i/; rarest /ɔɪ ʊ aʊ/.
- Commonest syllable shape: onset+no coda > onset+coda > no onset > onset=coda (rarest).

Two different ways to count the words in a sentence — take *"I came, I saw, I conquered"*:
- **Token**: count **every word**, including repeats. That sentence has 6 words total → **6 tokens**.
- **Type**: count each **different** word only **once**, even if it repeats. That sentence only has 4 distinct words (*I, came, saw, conquered*) — "I" shows up 3 times but only counts once → **4 types**.

- **Stem**: the base/root form you add endings to. *walk* is the stem of *walked, walking, walks*.
- **Lemma**: the single dictionary-entry form that stands in for a whole family of related forms, even irregular ones. *go* is the lemma covering *go, goes, going, went, gone* — "went" doesn't even look like "go," but it's still the same lemma.

## Loanword phonology
Borrowed words adapt to the **borrowing language's** phonemes/phonotactics (e.g. tone lost & stress added going into English; reversed going into Mandarin; clusters simplified).
- **Hawaiian**: (C)V only, no clusters/codas, no voiced stops, has nasals.
- ⚠️ All languages do this — pronouncing a loanword "correctly" can be hard/sound odd if your language lacks that sound (no [ʁ] in English "Notre Dame"; [eɪ] is simply more natural to English than [a]). Linguists describe this without endorsing "right/wrong."

## Interlinear glosses
3 lines: original language → morpheme-by-morpheme gloss → free translation. Grammatical labels in the gloss are small caps:
- **ASP** = aspect/aspectual marker — how an action unfolds over time (e.g. *is running* vs. *has run*).
- **PST** = past tense.
- **PL** = plural.
- **Romanization**: writing a language's words using the Latin/Roman alphabet, for a language that doesn't natively use it (e.g. writing Mandarin with letters instead of characters).
- **Stop-final syllable**: a syllable whose coda (last sound) is a stop consonant — p, t, k, b, d, or g. E.g. *cat* [kæt] is stop-final; *car* [kɑɹ] is not.
- **Agreement**: when one word changes form to match a feature (person/number/gender) of another word it's connected to — e.g. English "he run**s**" vs. "they run" (the verb agrees with the subject's number).
- Practice-exam ex. (Mandarin, "Mom yiwei I sick ASP" = "Mom is under the impression I'm sick"): assuming a fairly transparent romanization, this shows no stop-final syllables, an aspect marker ("le") with no 1-word English equivalent, no tense marking on verbs, and no agreement (person/number/gender) — **all** true.

## Machine translation & gender (Stanovsky et al. 2019) — ⚠️ not in slides, tested on practice exam
Translating gender-neutral English into Spanish (which marks gender on nouns *and* determiners) forces a gender choice → MT systems default to **stereotypical** choices (e.g. "nurse" = feminine), revealing bias baked into training data.

## Broad vs. Narrow Transcription
- **Narrow/phonetic** = max detail (e.g. [pʰɪn], marking aspiration).
- **Broad/phonemic** = phonemes only (e.g. /pɪn/, reader fills in the allophones themselves).
⚠️ Bracketed data from an unfamiliar language doesn't guarantee you know its actual pronunciation.

## Questions in English — ⚠️ not in the 4 decks (syntax topic), but on the practice exam
- **Yes/no questions**: made with **word order** + **do-support** — inserting a form of "do" when the sentence has no other helping verb. *"Lee read the book."* → *"**Did** Lee read the book?"*
- **Content/wh- questions**: the question word is **moved to the front** of the sentence, not left in its normal spot. *"Lee read the book."* → *"**What book** did Lee read?"* (not *"Lee read what book?"*).
