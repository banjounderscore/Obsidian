# Unit 1 — Introduction to Linguistics

## Why study language
- A defining feature of humanity — substance of social interaction, law, literature.
- **Productive/generative**: infinite new sentences from finite rules, not memorized.
- Applications: NLP, speech pathology, forensics, translation, language teaching.

## Facts
- ~**6,000 languages** spoken today; 90% of people speak only 10% of them.
- **WALS** (World Atlas of Language Structures) = database of structural properties across languages.
- **Endangered Languages Project** = tracks language vitality.

## Themes
1. Language is **systematic** — studied scientifically.
2. Systematic rules → **infinite ideas from finite means**.
3. Language **varies systematically** (not randomly).
4. Diverse across languages, but shares **universals**.
5. Much of language is **arbitrary**.
6. Grammar = complex, **largely implicit** rules.
7. Children **acquire** language without explicit teaching — innate.
8. All languages **change over time** (a theme, NOT a Hockett feature — see below).

> ⚠️ Since language is universal but explicit teaching isn't (tied to specific cultures/schooling), language *can't* depend on being taught.

## Competence vs. Performance
| | Competence | Performance |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Implicit knowledge of your language | What you actually say/do |
| Observable? | No | Yes |
| Example | No rule allows "the, the, the book" | You might still say it (a slip) |

## Speech Communication Chain
Think of what to say → choose words → order them → recall pronunciation → articulate → *(hearer)* perceive sounds → map to words/sentence → understand → infer speaker's meaning/intent.
- Only **~0.3 sec** gap between conversational turns — next utterance is planned before the other person finishes.

## Subfields
| Subfield | Studies | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Phonetics | Physical sound production/perception | *bet* vs. *beet* |
| Phonology | Sound patterning & context-rules | /s/ → [s] in *cats*, [z] in *dogs* |
| Morphology | Word formation | plural of *child*? |
| Syntax | Word order/sentence structure | *I picked up the package* |
| Semantics | Literal meaning | what does *pick up* mean? |
| Pragmatics | Meaning in context | an indirect "no" |
| Sociolinguistics | Social meaning of variation | accent → inferences about speaker |

## Ambiguity
- **Lexical** (homophony): one sound, 2+ words — [kɚnəl] = *kernel*/*colonel*.
- **Structural**: same words, 2+ parses — "river bank" vs. "financial bank."
- ⚠️ Not the same as synchronic/diachronic variation, descriptive/prescriptive, or allophone variation — those aren't about multiple *meanings*.

## Language ≠ Writing
- Spoken language is ephemeral, ~50k–200k years old; writing is only **~6,000 years old** (Mesopotamian bookkeeping).
- Some languages have no writing system at all.
- Everyone acquires spoken language (like walking); not everyone learns to write (a taught skill, like ballet).
- **Writing records language; writing is not language.**

## Prescriptive vs. Descriptive
| Prescriptive (should) | Descriptive (actually do) |
|---|---|
| "Don't say *ain't*." | "Some speakers use *ain't* to signal alienation from institutions." |
| "Don't end a sentence with a preposition." | "English adjectives precede nouns." |

- **Grammar** (to a linguist) = implicit knowledge of your language, not school rules.
- Linguistics is **descriptive**: no way of speaking is objectively "better"; prescriptive judgments often mask stereotypes about *speakers*, not real deficits in the language.
- ⚠️ *Reporting on* a prescriptive attitude ("many people think X is nonstandard") is itself **descriptive** — reporting a norm ≠ enforcing one.

## Hockett's Design Features
*(what distinguishes human language from animal communication)*

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vocal-auditory / gestural-visual | Spoken & heard, or signed & seen |
| Intentionality | Speakers mean to say what they say |
| Transitoriness | Ephemeral unless recorded |
| Interchangeability | Anything hearable is also sayable |
| Total feedback | You perceive your own speech/signing |
| Specialization | The signal exists *to* communicate |
| Semanticity | Specific forms map to specific meanings |
| Arbitrariness | Sound–meaning link is convention |
| Discreteness | Continuous sound mapped to discrete category |
| Displacement | Can discuss absent/imaginary/hypothetical things |
| Prevarication | Can be used to lie |
| Duality of patterning | Sounds → morphemes → words (two combinable levels) |
| Productivity | Infinite novel utterances |
| Learnability | Can learn a new language |
| Cultural transmission | You learn the language(s) around you |
| Reflexiveness | Language can describe itself |

> ⚠️ "Languages change over time" is **NOT** on this list — it's a theme, not a Hockett feature.

## Arbitrariness vs. Iconicity
- **Iconic** = sound depicts meaning (*buzz, boom* = onomatopoeia). **Arbitrary** = no such link (*whale, kowtow, yell*).
- **Bouba/kiki effect**: nonsense words reliably matched to shapes (round = *bouba*, spiky = *kiki*).
- **Tongue Pull Hypothesis**: high tongue position → higher pitch (pulls larynx up).
- **Frequency Code** (Ohala): high pitch ~ small; low pitch ~ large → explains why **diminutive suffixes** (*-ito, -y*) favor **high vowels** (Jurafsky).
- Trade-off: arbitrary = more expressive power; iconic = easier to learn/remember.

# Unit 2 — Phonetics: Consonants

## Vocal tract
Lungs → **larynx** (vocal folds = voicing) → oral/nasal cavity, shaped by articulators (lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, palate, velum, uvula, tongue, glottis).

![[Ling 2100 Notes-1790220720526.webp|453]]

## Airstream mechanisms
- **Pulmonic egressive** = the default (lungs push air out).
- **Non-pulmonic** (not in English): **clicks** (ingressive, tongue), **implosives** (ingressive, larynx lowers), **ejectives** (egressive, larynx raises; marked ʼ).

## Describing a consonant: Place / Manner / Voicing

**Place** (where the constriction happens):

| Place         | Definition                        | English examples |
| ------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Bilabial      | Both lips                         | p b m w          |
| Labiodental   | Lower lip + upper teeth           | f v              |
| Dental        | Tongue against upper teeth        | θ ð              |
| Alveolar      | Tongue against alveolar ridge     | t d n s z l      |
| Post-alveolar | Tongue just behind alveolar ridge | ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ        |
| Palatal       | Tongue body + hard palate         | j                |
| Velar         | Tongue body + velum (soft palate) | k g ŋ            |
| Glottal       | Vocal folds                       | h ʔ              |
| Retroflex     | Tongue tip curled back            | *not in English* |
| Uvular        | Tongue back + uvula               | *not in English* |
| Pharyngeal    | Tongue root + pharynx wall        | *not in English* |

**Manner** (how the airflow is shaped):

| Manner              | Definition                           | Example           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------- |
| Plosive/Stop        | Full closure, then release           | p b t d k g ʔ     |
| Nasal               | Oral closure, air through nose       | m n ŋ             |
| Fricative           | Narrow, turbulent passage            | f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h |
| Tap/Flap            | One quick tap                        | ɾ (*butter*)      |
| Approximant         | Close but no turbulence              | ɹ j (w)           |
| Lateral approximant | Air flows around sides of tongue     | l                 |
| Trill               | Articulator vibrates rapidly         | *not in English*  |
| Lateral fricative   | Turbulent air around sides of tongue | *not in English*  |

- **Liquid** = ɹ + l. **Glide** = w, j. **Affricate** = a stop released as a fricative in one segment (tʃ, dʒ).
- **Sonorants** (nasals/liquids/glides/vowels — always voiced) vs. **Obstruents** (stops/fricatives/affricates — contrast for voicing).

**Voicing**: voiceless {p t k f θ s ʃ h tʃ} vs. voiced (everything else).

## IPA vs. English spelling
- IPA = 1 symbol per sound; spelling isn't (*sing* = 4 letters/3 sounds; *text* = 4 letters/**5 sounds**).
- Spelling is ambiguous (*Leslie* = [lɛsli] or [lɛzli]); IPA never is.
- ⚠️ Judge a word's final *sound*, not its final letter (*Emma* and *Hannah* both end in [ə]).
- **Pros of all-IPA spelling**: easier phonics; instant pronunciation; disambiguates homographs (*close*/*close*).
- **Cons**: loses etymology info carried by silent letters; inconsistent records across accents; one accent's IPA misrepresents another's.

## Coarticulation & variation
- **Coarticulation**: neighboring sounds subtly affect each other (e.g. [d] in "bad guy" vs. "bad person").
- **Palatography**: paints the tongue to see where it touches the palate — studies articulation empirically.
- /ɹ/ varies by speaker: "molar/bunched" vs. "apical."

## Typology
- English's [θ, ð] (dental fricatives) are **typologically rare** cross-linguistically (Mielke 2017).
- More typologically common sounds are **acquired earlier** by children (stops before fricatives).
- **McGurk effect**: mismatched audio/visual input → sound is misperceived — speech perception is multi-modal.

## Preview of phonology
- **Minimal pair** = words differing by exactly one sound → proves **different phonemes**.
  - *sip/zip* → /s/≠/z/; *math/mass* → /θ/≠/s/ — English-specific (not true in Spanish).

# Unit 3 — Phonetics: Vowels

## Vowels vs. consonants
- Airflow unrestricted; inherently voiced.
- Defined by: **height** (high/mid/low), **backness** (front/central/back), **rounding**.

## English vowels
|      | Front   | Central | Back    |
| ---- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| High | i, ɪ    |         | u, ʊ    |
| Mid  | e/eɪ, ɛ | ə, ɚ, ʌ | o/oʊ, ɔ |
| Low  | æ       |         | ɑ       |

Diphthongs: **aɪ** (bite), **aʊ** (bout), **ɔɪ** (boy).
- **Schwa [ə]** = most common vowel in **unstressed** syllables.
- **Diphthong** = 2 vowel sounds in 1 syllable (vs. monophthong = 1 steady vowel).

## Tense vs. Lax
| Tense: i, u, e/eɪ, o | Lax: ɪ, ʊ, ɛ, ɔ, æ |
|---|---|
| Longer; can stand in an open syllable | Shorter; needs a coda (closed syllable) |

- ⚠️ **Rule**: lax vowels can't stand alone in an open syllable — "boo" [bu] ✓, "buh" [bʊ] ✗. So **[kʊ]** is illegal; **[kæk]**/**[kæθ]** are fine (closed syllables).

## Vowels & sociolinguistic variation
- Vowels are continuous, not discrete → **chain shifts** (one moves, others follow to preserve contrasts).
- **Synchronic** variation (across speakers, now) vs. **diachronic** (over time).

### Mergers
Often before a sonorant: Marry/merry/Mary, Pin/pen (South), Feel/fill (Appalachian), Cot/caught (young Americans).

### Shifts
- **Southern Vowel Shift**: DRESS raises/fronts, FACE lowers/backs, PRIZE monophthongizes (fading).
- **Northern Cities Shift**: Great Lakes region (also fading).
- **Great Vowel Shift** (1400s–1700s): explains English spelling/pronunciation mismatches.
- Evidence without recordings: **historical spellings** (*meat* ≠ *meet* in spelling, once different sounds) + **rhymes that no longer rhyme** (Shakespeare).

# Unit 4 — Phonology

## Phonetics vs. Phonology
**Phonetics** = how sounds are made/perceived. **Phonology** = the grammar of how sounds pattern & vary by context.

## Phoneme vs. Allophone
| | `/slashes/` = Phoneme | `[brackets]` = Allophone |
|---|---|---|
| = | Mental, contrastive category | Context-conditioned realization |
| Evidenced by | Minimal pairs | Complementary distribution |

- **Minimal pair**: 2 words, 1 sound different, different meaning → proves different **phonemes**.
- ⚠️ NOT allophones — allophones never distinguish meaning, so they can't form a minimal pair.

## Contrastive vs. Complementary Distribution
| | Contrastive | Complementary |
|---|---|---|
| Same environment? | Yes — meaning changes | Never overlap |
| Evidence | Minimal pairs | No minimal pairs |
| Conclusion | Different phonemes | Allophones (if *also* phonetically similar) |

> ⚠️ Complementary distribution alone ≠ allophones — also need **phonetic similarity** (see [h]/[ŋ] below).

## Formalizing a rule
$$/A/ \rightarrow [B] \; / \; X\_\_Y$$ — "/A/ becomes [B] after X, before Y."
- Template: /X/ has allophones [X], [Y]; [Y] occurs in a specific context; [X] = elsewhere (widest distribution = "elsewhere" case).
- Korean ex.: /s/ → [ʃ] / __[i]; [s] elsewhere.

## Key English Rules
**1. Aspiration** — /p, t, k/ → [pʰ, tʰ, kʰ] at the onset of a **stressed syllable**; [p, t, k] elsewhere (e.g. after /s/). Language-specific: **contrastive** in Thai, allophonic in English.

**2. Flapping** — /t/ → [tʰ] (stressed onset) / [ɾ] (between vowels, unstressed) / [ʔ] (word-final, some speakers) / [t] elsewhere.
- **Fortition** = strengthening (aspirating). **Lenition** = weakening (flapping). **Assimilation** = the flap voices to match surrounding vowels.

**3. L-velarization ("dark L")** — ⚠️ not in slides, but on the practice exam: /l/ → [ɫ] in **coda**; [l] elsewhere (onset).

**4. Nasal place assimilation** — "in-" → [ɪm]/[ɪn]/[ɪŋ], matching the place of the following consonant.

**5. Voicing assimilation** — past-tense/plural suffix matches the voicing of the preceding sound ([d]/[t], [z]/[s]); **epenthesis** adds [ə] when the stem already ends in a similar sound (*foxes* [fɑksəz]).

> ⚠️ "Singer" = **[sɪŋɚ]** — no [g] (contrast "finger" [fɪŋgɚ], same morpheme). Plural "singers" = **[sɪŋɚz]** — voicing assimilates to [z] after the voiced ɚ.

### [h]/[ŋ]: complementary distribution ≠ allophones
[h] (syllable-initial) and [ŋ] (coda) are in complementary distribution but **share no distinctive features** → most phonologists say NOT allophones.

## Natural classes & features
**Natural class** = sounds sharing features, so one rule covers the whole class (aspiration applies to all of {p, t, k}, not just /p/).
Features: [±voice], [±nasal], [±continuant], [±sonorant], plus place features (labial/coronal/dorsal).

## Allomorphs
Like allophones, but tied to a specific **morpheme** (need not be in complementary distribution; must be semantically equivalent).
- "a/an": [ə]/[ən] — **epenthesis** of [n] before a vowel.
- "in-": [m]/[n]/[ŋ] — nasal place assimilation.
- Past tense "-ed": [t]/[d]/[əd]. Plural "-s": [s]/[z]/[əz]. — both voicing assimilation + epenthesis.

## Syllable structure
**(Onset) – Nucleus – (Coda)**; only the Nucleus is required.
*cat* = Onset /k/, Nucleus /æ/, Coda /t/. English allows complex clusters — *strengths* is about as complex as it gets.

## Phonotactics
**Phonotactics** = which sound sequences are legal in a language (English: /spr-/ ✓, /spm-/ ✗).
- Cross-linguistic complexity: Simple (CV only) → Moderate (≤CCVC) → Complex (>CCVC).
- English, by word **type** (Yang 2016, CMU dict): commonest consonants /n l s t/; rarest /ʒ ð θ/ — though /ð/ is common by **token** (via "the"). Commonest vowels /ʌ ə ɪ i/; rarest /ɔɪ ʊ aʊ/.
- Commonest syllable shape: onset+no coda > onset+coda > no onset > onset=coda (rarest).

**Type vs. Token**: type = distinct word form; token = each occurrence. "I came, I saw, I conquered" = **6 tokens, 4 types**.
**Stem** = base form (*walk*). **Lemma** = dictionary citation form covering a whole paradigm (*go* covers *go/goes/went*).

## Loanword phonology
Borrowed words adapt to the **borrowing language's** phonemes/phonotactics (e.g. tone lost & stress added going into English; reversed going into Mandarin; clusters simplified).
- **Hawaiian**: (C)V only, no clusters/codas, no voiced stops, has nasals.
- ⚠️ All languages do this — pronouncing a loanword "correctly" can be hard/sound odd if your language lacks that sound (no [ʁ] in English "Notre Dame"; [eɪ] is simply more natural to English than [a]). Linguists describe this without endorsing "right/wrong."

## Interlinear glosses
3 lines: original language → morpheme-by-morpheme gloss (ASP/PST/PL = grammatical labels) → free translation.
- Practice-exam ex. (Mandarin, "Mom yiwei I sick ASP" = "Mom is under the impression I'm sick"): shows no stop-final syllables, an aspectual marker with no 1-word English equivalent, no tense marking, no agreement — **all** true.

## Machine translation & gender (Stanovsky et al. 2019) — ⚠️ not in slides, tested on practice exam
Translating gender-neutral English into Spanish (which marks gender on nouns *and* determiners) forces a gender choice → MT systems default to **stereotypical** choices (e.g. "nurse" = feminine), revealing bias baked into training data.

## Broad vs. Narrow Transcription
**Narrow/phonetic** = max detail (aspiration, flaps). **Broad/phonemic** = phonemes only, reader fills in allophones.
⚠️ Bracketed data from an unfamiliar language doesn't guarantee you know its actual pronunciation.

# Self-Check
1. [k]/[kʰ] in English? → Allophones (no minimal pairs; same aspiration rule).
2. [ɸ] word-initial / [f] elsewhere, no minimal pairs → allophones; /f/ → [ɸ] / #__, [f] elsewhere.
3. Why is "streen" legal but "ptreen" illegal in English? → Onsets allow s+stop+liquid, not stop+stop.
4. Allophone vs. allomorph? → Allomorph is tied to one meaningful morpheme; allophone is just a phonetic variant.
5. Why isn't complementary distribution alone enough for allophones? → Also need phonetic similarity ([h]/[ŋ] counterexample).
