# Unit 1 — Introduction to Linguistics

## Why study language?
- Language is a **defining feature of humanity** — it's the substance of social interaction, literature, law, and historical record.
- It's **productive / generative**: we produce and understand infinite novel sentences we've never heard before (not memorized).
  - *"Each person's life is lived as a series of conversations." — Deborah Tannen*
- Applications: NLP (chatbots, speech-to-text, summarization), speech pathology, language policy, forensic linguistics, language & law, language & medicine, advertising, translation/localization, human-computer interaction, language teaching.

## Surprising facts (typology)
- **~6,000 languages** spoken today; ~90% of people speak only ~10% of them.
- Some languages have a verb tense used just for gossip (Turkish).
- Some languages put Object before Subject (OVS-type word orders exist).
- Some languages don't mark tense on verbs at all.
- **WALS = World Atlas of Language Structures** — a database of **structural (grammatical/phonological) properties of the world's languages**, used to compare typology across languages (wals.info).
- **Endangered Languages Project** — tracks language vitality/endangerment.

## Themes of the study of language
1. Language is **systematic** — can be studied scientifically at every level (sounds, words, sentences, discourse).
2. Systematic rules let us express **infinite ideas from finite means**.
3. Language **varies systematically** — by person, region, situation — and this variation is itself statistically patterned (not "random").
4. Languages are **diverse**, but share **striking universals**.
5. Much of language is **arbitrary** (unpredictable from general principles).
6. Grammar is governed by **complex, largely implicit rules** — a linguist's job is to make the implicit explicit.
7. Children **acquire** language without explicit teaching — it's **innate**, more like learning to walk than learning ballet/bicycling.
8. **All languages change over time** (this is a *theme*, not literally one of Hockett's named design features — see note below ⚠️).

> ⚠️ **Exam trap (reasoning question)**: themes 7's "unlearned" and the fact that **language is universal across humans** are logically connected, not just two random facts — explicit instruction would necessarily be tied to specific cultures/education systems (not every culture teaches language explicitly), so if language depended on explicit teaching, it wouldn't be universal. Since it *is* universal, it *can't* depend on explicit instruction.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** "Languages change over time" is a *theme* of linguistics, but it is **not** one of Hockett's design features as presented in this course (see Hockett list below). If a question asks "which of these is NOT a Hockett feature," language change is a good candidate for the odd one out.

## Competence vs. Performance
| | Competence | Performance |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Implicit/idealized **knowledge** of how your language works | What you actually **say and do** |
| Observable? | No — hidden, internal | Yes — directly observable |
| Example | Underlying, "correct" grammatical rule (no thrice-repeated determiners) | "The, the, uh, the book" (a slip is performance, not evidence about competence) |

## Speech Communication Chain
How a message travels from one mind to another — **speaker side**, then **hearer side**:
1. Think of what to say.
2. Pick out words to say it.
3. Put the words in order.
4. Remember how to pronounce the words.
5. Say the words (in order) with your mouth.
6. *(Hearer)* Hear the sounds.
7. Map sounds to words; words to a sentence with structure and meaning.
8. Understand what the speaker said.
9. Consider what the speaker could have said instead, but didn't.
10. Draw inferences about what the speaker meant, believes, and wants.

- All of this happens **astonishingly fast** in real time: there's a gap of only **~0.3 seconds** between conversational turns.
- Speakers **plan their next utterance before** the other person has even finished talking.

## Subfields of linguistics (what you implicitly know)
| Subfield | Studies... | Example from slides |
|---|---|---|
| **Phonetics** | Physical production/perception of speech sounds | How is *bet* different from *beet*? |
| **Phonology** | The grammar of sound patterns; how pronunciation shifts by context | We don't pronounce /pt/ in sequence in *pterodactyl*; "s" is [s] in *cats* but [z] in *dogs* |
| **Morphology** | Word structure/formation | Past tense of *walk*? Plural of *child*? What does *Swiftmas* mean? |
| **Syntax** | Word order / sentence structure | *I picked up the package at eight* vs. ungrammatical *\*I picked the package at eight up* |
| **Semantics** | Literal meaning | What does *pick up* mean? How does tense relate to *at eight*? |
| **Pragmatics** | Meaning in context / conversational contribution | "I picked up the package at eight" as an indirect **no** to "do you want me to get it?" |
| **Sociolinguistics** | Social meaning of variation | What do you infer about someone from their accent? |

## Ambiguity
- **Ambiguity**: when a single word or string of words has more than one possible meaning.
- **Lexical ambiguity (homophony)**: two different words that happen to sound identical — e.g. **[kɚnəl]** = "kernel" (corn) or "colonel" (military rank).
- **Structural ambiguity**: the same string of words can be grammatically parsed two different ways — e.g. "river bank" (edge of a river) vs. "financial bank" (institution); the headline "Pope's Baby Steps on Gays" (did the Pope take small/cautious steps, or step on babies who are gay?).
- ⚠️ **Exam trap**: ambiguity is a distinct concept from — don't confuse it with — **synchronic/diachronic variation** (about *change over time*), **descriptive/prescriptive** (about *norms*), or **contrastive/complementary distribution** like aspirated vs. unaspirated allophones (about *phonological conditioning*, not multiple meanings).

## Language is *not* writing
- No one knows exactly how old spoken language is (it's ephemeral) — estimates **~50,000–200,000 years**.
- Humans expanded out of Africa ~60,000–90,000 years ago; decorative beads (implying symbolic communication, so probably language) ~70,000 years ago.
- First stone tools (Homo habilis): ~2.6 million years ago.
- **Writing is only ~6,000 years old** — likely began as trade/bookkeeping records on clay tablets in Mesopotamia.
- Some languages (e.g., many in rural Papua New Guinea) have **no writing system** at all.
- Everyone acquires spoken/signed language (like walking); not everyone learns to read/write (like ballet) — a **learned, taught skill**, unlike core language.
- **Writing records language; writing is not itself language.**

## Prescriptive vs. Descriptive
| Prescriptive | Descriptive |
|---|---|
| Rules about what people **SHOULD** do | Rules about what people **ACTUALLY** do |
| "People shouldn't say *ain't*." | "Some speakers use *ain't* to signal alienation from institutions." |
| "Don't end a sentence with a preposition." | "English adjectives precede nouns (*red dress*, not *dress red*)." |
| Value judgment | Value-neutral, scientific observation |

- **"Grammar"** to a linguist = each speaker's largely unconscious/implicit knowledge of how their language works — **not** what's taught in school.
- Linguistics is a fundamentally **descriptive** endeavor: no ways of speaking are objectively "better." Prescriptive judgments often mask **stereotypes about the people** who speak that way, even though the speakers may lack the cultural power to have their variety declared "correct."
- **Practice — descriptive or prescriptive?**
  - *"Young people are ruining language."* → prescriptive (value judgment about decline)
  - *"Many speakers consider 'ain't' prescriptively nonstandard."* → **descriptive** (it's a factual/sociological observation *about* a prescriptive attitude — reporting on a norm is not the same as enforcing it!)
  - *"It is illogical to use multiple negation."* → prescriptive
  - *"A speaker who uses multiple negation in professional contexts may attract negative social evaluations."* → descriptive (reporting a social fact)

## Hockett's Design Features of Language
*(Charles Hockett — features distinguishing human language from other communication systems, e.g., animal signals)*

| Feature | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Vocal-auditory / gestural-visual channel | Produced vocally & heard, OR signed & seen | Speech vs. sign languages |
| **Intentionality** | People *intend* to say what they say | (Contrast: unintentional signals) |
| **Transitoriness** (rapid fading) | Speech is ephemeral unless recorded | Writing ≠ language because it's *not* transitory |
| **Interchangeability** | Anything you can hear, you can also say (and vice versa) | Contrast: only queen ants emit a certain chemical signal |
| **Total feedback** | Speakers hear their own speech; signers see their own signs | — |
| **Specialization** | The signal exists *specifically* to communicate, not as a side effect | Contrast: a panting dog signals heat, but panting's *purpose* is cooling, not communication |
| **Semanticity** | Specific forms map to specific meanings | — |
| **Arbitrariness** | The sound–meaning link is (mostly) a matter of convention | *dog / perro / chien / Hund* — no non-arbitrary reason to prefer one |
| **Discreteness** | Continuous physical variation is mapped onto discrete mental categories | Different speakers' [d]'s are all "the same" phoneme mentally |
| **Displacement** | Can talk about things absent, imaginary, false, or hypothetical | Unlike pointing, which needs the referent present |
| **Prevarication** | Language can be used to lie | — |
| **Duality of patterning** | Made of discrete units at two levels: meaningless sounds combine into meaningful morphemes, which combine into words/sentences | sentence → words → morphemes → sounds |
| **Productivity** | Can produce/understand infinite novel utterances | — |
| **Learnability** | You can learn a new language | — |
| **Cultural transmission** | You learn the language(s) around you (not fully innate content) | — |
| **Reflexiveness** | Language can be used to talk about language itself (metalanguage) | This very sentence! |

## Arbitrariness vs. Iconicity (Sound Symbolism)
- Sound–meaning pairing is **mostly arbitrary** but not entirely.
- **Iconicity**: a non-accidental, depictive relation between sound and meaning.
  - *Onomatopoeia* is the clearest case: "buzz," "boom" (sound mimics referent's sound).
  - "Kowtow," "whale," "yell" are **not** iconic (arbitrary); "buzz" **is** iconic.
- **Bouba/kiki effect**: people reliably match the nonsense word *bouba* to round shapes and *kiki* to spiky shapes — a cross-linguistic iconic tendency (sound shape ~ visual shape).
- **Tongue Pull Hypothesis** (Peter Ladefoged): high vowels (tongue high, e.g. [i]) are produced with **higher pitch** than low vowels ([ɑ]) because a raised tongue pulls the larynx upward.
- **Frequency Code** (John Ohala): we associate **high-pitched sounds with small entities**, low-pitched sounds with larger entities.
- **Jurafsky**: combining these two ideas explains why **diminutive suffixes** (*-ito, -ette, -y* as in *doggy, perrito*) cross-linguistically favor **high (small-sounding) vowels** — non-arbitrary sound symbolism.
- ⚠️ **Practice-exam note**: this exact chain — Tongue Pull Hypothesis → Frequency Code → diminutive suffixes use high vowels — isn't spelled out verbatim in the 4 decks, but the practice exam gives the full reasoning in the question itself and tests whether you can follow it to the conclusion (diminutive suffixes favor high vowels).
- Trade-off: **arbitrary** signs → greater expressive power but harder to learn/memorize; **iconic** signs → easier to learn/remember but limited expressive range. Language balances both pressures.

# Unit 2 — Phonetics: Consonants

## The vocal tract
Air from the lungs passes the **larynx** (vocal folds — the source of **voicing**), then through the **oral and/or nasal cavities**, shaped by **articulators**: lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, velum (soft palate), uvula, tongue (tip/blade/body/root), and glottis.

![[Ling 2100 Notes-1790220720526.webp|453]]

## Airstream mechanisms
- **Pulmonic egressive** = air pushed out from the lungs — the default for virtually all speech sounds in virtually all languages.
- **Non-pulmonic consonants** (⚠️ gap-fill — slides mention these as "fun facts" but don't define the 3 types). The IPA chart gives each its own symbol set by place:

| Clicks | Voiced implosives | Ejectives |
|---|---|---|
| ʘ Bilabial | ɓ Bilabial | pʼ Bilabial |
| ǀ Dental | ɗ Dental/alveolar | tʼ Dental/alveolar |
| ǃ (Post)alveolar | ʄ Palatal | kʼ Velar |
| ǂ Palatoalveolar | ɠ Velar | sʼ Alveolar fricative |
| ǁ Alveolar lateral | ʛ Uvular | |

  - **Clicks**: velaric ingressive airstream (air drawn in between two closures in the mouth) — found phonemically in languages like Zulu, Xhosa.
  - **Implosives**: glottalic ingressive airstream (larynx lowers while a closure is held) — found in Sindhi, Vietnamese, etc.
  - **Ejectives**: glottalic egressive airstream (larynx raises to compress air behind a closure, marked with an apostrophe **ʼ**) — found in many Native American, Caucasian, and African languages.
  - None of these three are part of English.

## Describing a consonant: Place, Manner, Voicing
Every consonant is defined by **3 dimensions**:

**1. Place of articulation** — *where* in the vocal tract the constriction happens (aligned with the full 11-place IPA chart below):

| Place         | Articulators                                  | English examples                                                                |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bilabial      | both lips                                     | p, b, m, w                                                                      |
| Labiodental   | lower lip + upper teeth                       | f, v                                                                            |
| Dental        | tongue tip/blade + upper teeth                | θ, ð                                                                            |
| Alveolar      | tongue tip/blade + alveolar ridge             | t, d, n, s, z, l                                                                |
| Post-alveolar | tongue blade behind the alveolar ridge        | ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ                                                                    |
| Retroflex     | tongue tip curled back toward the hard palate | none in English (some speakers' /ɹ/ is retroflex-like, but not phonemically so) |
| Palatal       | tongue body + hard palate                     | j                                                                               |
| Velar         | tongue body + velum (soft palate)             | k, g, ŋ (w has a secondary velar articulation)                                  |
| Uvular        | tongue back + uvula                           | none in English (e.g. French/German "r" [ʁ], Arabic [q])                        |
| Pharyngeal    | tongue root + pharynx wall                    | none in English (e.g. Arabic [ħ, ʕ])                                            |
| Glottal       | vocal folds                                   | h, ʔ                                                                            |

**2. Manner of articulation** — *how* the airflow is shaped (⚠️ gap-fill — not spelled out on slides, needed for e.g. "fricative" definitions; aligned with the full 8-manner IPA chart below):

| Manner                  | Definition                                                                         | English examples                                                     |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Plosive**             | Complete closure of the airflow, then a release                                    | p, b, t, d, k, g, ʔ                                                  |
| **Nasal**               | Complete oral closure, but the velum lowers so air escapes through the nose        | m, n, ŋ                                                              |
| **Trill**               | The articulator vibrates rapidly, striking the place of articulation several times | none in English (e.g. Spanish "rr" [r], uvular [ʀ] in French/German) |
| **Tap or Flap**         | A single, quick contact of the articulator against the place of articulation       | ɾ (English allophone of /t, d/, as in "butter," "ladder")            |
| **Fricative**           | Air forced through a **narrow, turbulent** passage — continuous "hissy" noise      | f, v, θ, ð, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, h                                            |
| **Lateral fricative**   | Turbulent airflow around the side(s) of the tongue                                 | none in English (e.g. Welsh "ll" [ɬ])                                |
| **Approximant**         | Articulators approach each other but don't create turbulence; vowel-like           | ɹ, j (w is a doubly-articulated approximant — see chart note)        |
| **Lateral approximant** | Air flows freely around the side(s) of the tongue, with no turbulence              | l                                                                    |

> **Note on informal cover terms used elsewhere in these notes:** some older/simplified terminology still appears in other sections (and in many textbooks) alongside these 8 official manners — **Liquid** = Approximant + Lateral approximant together (ɹ, l); **Glide/semivowel** = the non-syllabic Approximants w, j; **Affricate** = a Plosive released as a Fricative in one segment (tʃ, dʒ), not its own row on the official IPA chart. Know both the official 8-manner system and these informal groupings — the practice exam may use either.

**3. Voicing** — whether the vocal folds vibrate during the sound:
- **Voiceless**: p, t, k, f, θ, s, ʃ, h, tʃ
- **Voiced**: b, d, g, v, ð, z, ʒ, dʒ, m, n, ŋ, l, ɹ, w, j (all sonorants are voiced by default)

- **Sonorants vs. Obstruents** (needed for the vowel-mergers section too):
  - **Sonorants** = nasals, liquids, glides, vowels — continuous voicing/resonance, no major airflow obstruction.
  - **Obstruents** = stops, fricatives, affricates — obstruct airflow; contrast for voicing.

## IPA vs. English orthography
- IPA holds to **1 symbol = 1 sound (phone)**; English spelling does not.
  - *"Sing"* = 4 letters, 3 sounds: [sɪŋ].
  - *"Tax"* = 3 letters, 4 sounds: [tæks].
  - *"Text"* = 4 letters, **5 sounds**: [tɛkst] (t-ɛ-k-s-t).
  - Spelling is **ambiguous** (e.g. "Leslie" could be [lɛsli] or [lɛzli]); IPA never is.
- **Common misconception to avoid**: judging "the sound something ends in" by its *spelling*, not its pronunciation (e.g., "Emma" ends in the letter A and the sound [ə]; "Hannah" also ends in the sound [ə] despite ending in the letter "h").
- **Pros of switching English spelling to IPA**: easier phonics for kids; instantly know pronunciation of names; disambiguates homographs like *close* (verb) vs. *close* (adjective).
- **Cons**: loses information from silent letters about etymology/meaning; harder to keep consistent records (names/products) across accents; doesn't accommodate the fact that different English accents pronounce the same word differently, so a single "IPA spelling" would itself misrepresent someone else's accent.

## Coarticulation & fine-grained variation
- Even the "same" IPA symbol varies subtly by context: [d] in "bad guy" vs. "bad person" differ slightly due to **coarticulation** (articulation is influenced by neighboring sounds).
- [s] tends to be articulated slightly more forward or backward depending on the speaker (with a rough — not universal — gender-linked tendency in some populations), raising the question of physiological vs. social causes.
- **/ɹ/ (the English "r")** varies in articulation strategy across speakers: **"molar/bunched"** (back sides of tongue against back teeth, tip free) vs. **"apical"** (tongue tip at the alveolar ridge or retroflexed).
- **Palatography**: a method for studying articulation empirically — paint the tongue with a charcoal/olive-oil mixture, have the speaker say a word, then photograph where the roof of the mouth is stained, showing exactly where the tongue touched.

## Typology of consonants
- Cross-linguistic frequency data (Mielke 2017, *Journal of the IPA*) shows what % of the world's languages have each sound in their inventory.
- **English is typologically unusual for having dental fricatives** [θ, ð] — these are cross-linguistically rare sounds.
- Sounds that are **more typologically common are acquired earlier** by children (e.g., **stops are more common & acquired younger than fricatives**) — WALS data on this.
- **McGurk effect**: when visual (lip movement) and auditory input mismatch (e.g., seeing a person say [v] but hearing [b]), listeners misperceive the sound — proof that speech perception is **multi-modal** (audio + visual), not audio-only.

## Preview of phonology (from the consonants deck)
- **Minimal pair**: a pair of words differing by exactly **one sound**, used to prove two sounds are **different phonemes**.
  - *sip/zip* → /s/ ≠ /z/ in English. *math/mass* → /θ/ ≠ /s/ in English.
  - Not minimal pairs: *fawn/vawn* (not both real words), *thumb/thug* (differ in final sound, not one clean single-segment swap... actually differs in coda: /m/ vs /g/, so it IS arguably a minimal pair for final consonant, but "vawn" fails since it's not an English word), *ship/snip* (differ by more than one sound — inserted /n/), *sap/sip* (differ in the **vowel**, not a consonant — still a valid minimal pair, just for vowels, not consonants!).
  - **Language-specific**: *sip/zip* only proves /s/≠/z/ in **English**. In Spanish, [s] and [z] are **not** distinct phonemes (no minimal pairs distinguish them there).

# Unit 3 — Phonetics: Vowels

## What makes vowels different from consonants
- Air flows **unrestricted** (no major constriction), unlike consonants.
- **Inherently voiced** (unless whispering).
- Distinguished along **3 continuous dimensions**:
  1. **Tongue height** (high/mid/low)
  2. **Tongue front/backness** (front/central/back)
  3. **Lip rounding** (rounded/unrounded)

## English vowel chart (with keywords)
| Height \ Backness | Front | Central | Back |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | i (beet), ɪ (bit) | | u (boot), ʊ (book) |
| Mid | e/eɪ (bait), ɛ (bet) | ə (sofa), ɚ (bert), ʌ (but) | o/oʊ (boat), ɔ (bought) |
| Low | æ (bat) | | ɑ (bot) |

Diphthongs (a glide within one syllable): **aɪ** (bite), **aʊ** (bout), **ɔɪ** (boy)

> Note: the real `ipachart.com` chart draws this space as a **trapezoid, not a rectangle**, since the tongue has less room to move at the bottom (low vowels) than at the top (high) — the table above keeps the same height×backness logic, just squared off for readability. /ə, ɚ, ʌ, ɑ/ are extra central/back vowels; the slides' explicit tense/lax lists only classify the 4 tense and 5 lax vowels named just below.

- **Schwa [ə]**: the **most common vowel in unstressed English syllables**; a mid-central, "neutral" vowel.
  - [ˈsofə] *sofa*, [bəˈniθ] *beneath*, [ˈpɛnsəl] *pencil*, [ˈmɛməɹi] *memory*
- **Diphthong**: two adjacent vowel sounds ("glide") within **one syllable**: [pɹaɪz] *prize*, [haʊs] *house*, [bɔɪ] *boy*, [feɪs] *face* (sometimes [fes]).
  - Contrast with **monophthongs** (single, steady-state vowel quality).
  - Some languages (e.g. Japanese) have **no diphthongs** in their inventory.

## Tense vs. Lax vowels
| Tense | Lax |
|---|---|
| [i, u, e/eɪ, o] — *beet, boot, bait, boat* | [ɪ, ʊ, ɛ, ɔ, æ] — *bit, book, bet, bought, bat* |
| Longer duration | Shorter duration |
| Can appear in **open syllables** (ending in the vowel, no coda) | In English, **must be followed by a consonant** (closed syllables only) |

- **Phonotactic constraint**: English disallows lax vowels in open syllables:
  - "bee" [bi] ✓ but not "bih" [bɪ] ✗
  - "bay" [beɪ] ✓ but not "beh" [bɛ] ✗
  - "boo" [bu] ✓ but not "buh" [bʊ] ✗
  - This is *why* it would sound odd to name a child "Beh" (like *Ben* minus the /n/) — [bɛ] alone is phonotactically illegal in English.
  - **Worked example (nonce syllables)**: is **[kʊ]** a phonotactically legal syllable in English? **No** — [ʊ] is lax with no coda (open syllable), same illegal pattern as *"buh"* [bʊ] above. Compare **[kæk]** or **[kæθ]** — both legal, because the lax vowel [æ] is followed by a coda consonant (closed syllable).

## Vowels & sociolinguistic variation
- Vowels are **more continuous** than consonants (a consonant either is bilabial or isn't; a vowel can be realized at infinitely many points in a continuous articulatory space).
- Vowels **compete for space** in the mouth — if one vowel shifts position, others often shift too, to preserve contrasts (**chain shifts**).
- Variation across speakers/regions/accents at one point in time = **synchronic variation**; change in a language over time = **diachronic variation**. (Synchronic variation is essentially a "snapshot" of diachronic change in progress.)

### Vowel mergers
Two historically/individually distinct vowels collapse into one, often conditioned by a following **sonorant** (nasal or liquid):

| Merger | Example | Where common |
|---|---|---|
| Marry/merry/Mary | [mæɹi, mɛɹi, meɪɹi] → often all merge | Common in American English (not universal) |
| Pin/pen | [pɪn, pɛn] → merge | American South, African American English |
| Feel/fill | [fil, fɪl] → merge | Appalachian English, African American English |
| Cot/caught | [kɑt, kɔt] → merge | Common among young Americans |

### Vowel shifts (chain shifts)
- **Southern Vowel Shift** (historically American South, e.g. Georgia; now fading, esp. among young/urban/educated speakers):
  - DRESS [ɛ] moves higher/fronter
  - FACE [eɪ] moves lower/backer
  - PRIZE [aɪ] becomes **monophthongal** [aː] ("Ah'm" for "I'm")
- **Northern Cities Vowel Shift**: a chain shift historically found around the Great Lakes region — also fading.
- **The Great Vowel Shift** (~1400s–1700s): a major historical shift in English long vowels; explains many English spelling/pronunciation mismatches (English spelling froze roughly pre-shift).
- **How do we know about historical vowel shifts with no recordings?**
  1. **Historical spellings** (before standardized orthography) — e.g., "meat" and "meet" are spelled differently despite now both being [mit], because they used to be pronounced differently ([mɛt] vs. [met]).
  2. **Poetry that was meant to rhyme** but no longer does in modern pronunciation — e.g., Shakespeare rhyming "increase" with "memory," or "die" (now [daɪ]) with a word that would have been [di] at the time.

# Unit 4 — Phonology

## Phonetics vs. Phonology
- **Phonetics**: how sounds are physically made & perceived.
- **Phonology**: the (largely implicit) **grammar** governing (a) which sounds pattern together in a language, and (b) how pronunciation of a sound varies **predictably** by context.

## Phoneme vs. Allophone
- **Phoneme**: the smallest **meaningful/contrastive** unit of sound in a *particular language* — a mental category. Written in **/slashes/**.
- **Allophone**: a context-conditioned phonetic realization of a phoneme. Written in **[square brackets]**.

**Notation key — / / vs. [ ]:**

| | `/slashes/` | `[brackets]` |
|---|---|---|
| Level | **Phonemic** (broad transcription) | **Phonetic** (narrow transcription) |
| Represents | The abstract sound category a speaker mentally treats as "one sound" | The actual, physical realization — all the fine phonetic detail included |
| Example | /p/ (one phoneme) | [pʰ] vs [p] (two different allophones of that phoneme) |
| Analogy | The **word** you'd say | Everything you actually did to **produce** it |

Rule of thumb: **/slashes/ = phoneme (category)**, **[brackets] = phone/allophone (concrete sound)**. E.g., /pɪn/ is realized as [pʰɪn] in English. (A third convention, ⟨angle brackets⟩, marks spelling/orthography — not used for phonemes or phones.)
- **Minimal pair**: two words differing by exactly one sound in the same position, with different meanings → proves the two sounds are **different phonemes** (contrastive).
  - e.g., [ʃi], [si], [wi], [ni] ("she, see, we, knee") — same context (before [i]), different meanings → /ʃ/, /s/, /w/, /n/ are all distinct English phonemes.
  - ⚠️ **Exam trap**: a minimal pair is distinguished by two different **phonemes** — NOT two different allophones. (Allophones, by definition, don't change meaning, so two allophones could never make a minimal pair.)

## Contrastive vs. Complementary Distribution
| | Contrastive distribution | Complementary distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Two sounds appear in the **same environment** and change meaning | Two sounds **never** appear in the same environment — each has its own predictable context |
| Evidenced by | Minimal pairs | No minimal pairs; environments are mutually exclusive |
| Conclusion | **Different phonemes** | Usually **allophones of one phoneme** (if also phonetically similar — see caveat below) |
| Example | /s/ vs /z/ in English (*sip/zip*) | [p] vs [pʰ] in English — no minimal pairs |

> ⚠️ **Important caveat (exam-relevant):** complementary distribution alone is **not sufficient** to prove two sounds are allophones — they also need to be **phonetically similar** (share most distinctive features). See the [h]/[ŋ] case below.

## Formalizing a phonological rule
General schema:
$$/A/ \rightarrow [B] \; / \; X\_\_Y$$
"Phoneme /A/ becomes allophone [B] in the environment after X and before Y."
- `/ /` = phonemes; `[ ]` = allophones; `→` = "becomes"; `/` = "in the environment of"; `___` = where the target sound sits.
- Standard **general rule template**:
  1. Phoneme /X/ has allophones [X] and [Y].
  2. Allophone [Y] occurs in a *specific*, describable context.
  3. Allophone [X] occurs **elsewhere** (the elsewhere/default case — determine which allophone is "elsewhere" by checking which one appears in the widest variety of contexts).

### Worked example: Korean [s]/[ʃ]
If [s] and [ʃ] are allophones of /s/ in a language, and [ʃ] appears only before [i] while [s] appears everywhere else:
$$/s/ \rightarrow [\text{ʃ}] \; / \; \_\_[i]$$

## Key English phonological rules
### 1. Aspiration
- Data: [spɑt] *spot*, [pʰɑt] *pot*, [pʰæt] *pat*, [spæt] *spat*, [tʰɑp] *top*, [pɹɑp] *prop*, [ˈpɹɑpɚ] *proper*.
- No minimal pairs for [p]/[pʰ] → **not contrastive** → allophones of /p/ (the same logic extends to /t/ and /k/).
- **Rule**: Voiceless stops /p, t, k/ are aspirated **[pʰ, tʰ, kʰ] at the onset of a stressed syllable**; unaspirated **[p, t, k]** elsewhere (e.g. after /s/, or in an unstressed syllable's onset).
  $$\text{/p, t, k/} \rightarrow [\text{pʰ, tʰ, kʰ}] \; / \; \_\_ \, \sigma_{\text{stressed}}$$
- This is why the standard IPA chart symbols look "off" for English: since English /p, t, k/ are usually cited in their aspirated (elsewhere-marked) form, unaspirated [p, t, k] can sound almost like [b, d, g] to English ears (both lack aspiration) even though voicing, not aspiration, is what English uses to distinguish /p/ from /b/.
- **This rule is language-specific**: in **Thai**, [t] and [tʰ] are in **contrastive** distribution (different phonemes, minimal pairs exist) — aspiration is phonemic in Thai but only allophonic in English.

### 2. Flapping (Tapping) & Glottalization
- Data: [æɾəm] *atom*, [əˈtʰɑmɪk] *atomic*, [ˈleɪɾɚ] *later*, [tʰuɾɚ] *tutor*, [tuˈtʰɔɹiəl] *tutorial*, [leɪt]/[leɪʔ] *late*, [sɛt]/[sɛʔ] *set*, [ˈsɛɾɪŋ] *setting*.
- **Rule** (English /t/ has (at least) 4 surface allophones):
  - **[tʰ]** at the onset of a stressed syllable
  - **[ɾ]** (flap/tap) between two vowels, when *not* at the onset of a stressed syllable
  - **[ʔ]** (glottal stop) word-finally, for some speakers
  - **[t]** elsewhere (the "plain"/default realization)
- **Types of phonological processes** these illustrate:
  - **Fortition** (strengthening): aspirating /t/ at the start of a stressed syllable.
  - **Lenition** (weakening): realizing /t/ as [ɾ] between vowels — weaker because it's less distinctive from its voiced vocalic surroundings.
  - **Assimilation**: the flap [ɾ] is voiced because it takes on the voicing of the surrounding (voiced) vowels.

### 3. L-velarization ("dark L" / "light L") — ⚠️ Gap-fill (not in slides at all, but shows up on the practice exam)
- Data: "leaf" [lif] (onset), "feel" [fiɫ] (coda), "light" [laɪt] (onset), "tile" [taɪəɫ] (coda), "lead" [lid] (onset), "deal" [diəɫ] (coda), "felt" [fɛɫt] (coda before another consonant), "alive" [əlaɪv] (onset, intervocalic).
- [l] (clear/light L) and [ɫ] (velarized/"dark" L — back of tongue raised toward velum) are **allophones** of /l/ — not contrastive (no minimal pairs; the choice is fully predictable from position).
- **Rule**: /l/ → [ɫ] in **syllable coda** position (after the vowel); [l] elsewhere (syllable onset, before a vowel).
  $$/l/ \rightarrow [\text{ɫ}] \; / \; V\_\_(\text{C or } \#)$$

### 4. Nasal place assimilation
- Data: [ɪntʰɑləɹənt] *intolerant*, [ɪmbɑləns] *imbalance*, [ɪŋkʰəɹɛkt] *incorrect*.
- The prefix "in-" surfaces as **[ɪn]**, **[ɪm]**, or **[ɪŋ]** depending on the following consonant's place of articulation.
- **Rule**: a nasal **assimilates to the place of articulation** of the following consonant (labial before labials → [m]; velar before velars → [ŋ]; alveolar elsewhere → [n]).

### 5. Voicing assimilation (past tense & plural suffixes)
- Past tense: [pleɪd] *played* (voiced stem → [d]), [dudəld] *doodled* ([d]), [wɑlkt] *walked* (voiceless stem → [t]), [pɹɑpt] *propped* ([t]).
- Plural: [dɔgz] *dogs* ([z] after voiced), [kʰæts] *cats* ([s] after voiceless), [biz] *bees* ([z]), [fɑksəz] *foxes* ([əz], with **epenthesis** — see below — after a sibilant).
- **Rule**: the suffix consonant **assimilates in voicing** to the preceding sound (voiced after voiced sounds, voiceless after voiceless sounds); an extra vowel is **epenthesized** (inserted) when the stem already ends in a sound too similar to the suffix (a sibilant, for [-s]/[-z]) to pronounce them as a cluster.

> ⚠️ **Transcription trap**: "singer" is **[sɪŋɚ]** — no [g] after [ŋ] (contrast "finger" **[fɪŋgɚ]**, which *does* keep a [g], because that [ŋg] cluster is inside one morpheme, unlike "sing+er" across a morpheme boundary). And since the agentive **-er/[ɚ]** ending is voiced, the plural **-s** on a word like "singers" must voicing-assimilate to **[z]**: **[sɪŋɚz]**, not [sɪŋɚs]. A transcription like *[sɪŋŋɚɹs]* is wrong on three counts: a stray extra [g]/[ŋ], a stray [ɹ], and a voiceless [s] where voicing assimilation requires [z].

### The [h]/[ŋ] caveat — complementary distribution ≠ automatically allophones
- [h] and [ŋ] are in **complementary distribution** in English: [h] occurs only **syllable-initially**; [ŋ] occurs only as (part of) a **syllable coda**. No minimal pairs.
- **But** most phonologists say [h] and [ŋ] are **not** allophones of a single phoneme, because true allophones are normally **phonetically similar** (share most distinctive features) — and [h] (glottal fricative) and [ŋ] (velar nasal) **share essentially no distinctive features**.
- **Takeaway rule for the exam**: allophones require *both* (1) complementary distribution *and* (2) phonetic similarity. Complementary distribution alone can be a coincidence.

## Natural classes & distinctive features
- A **natural class** = a group of sounds sharing distinctive features, so that a single phonological rule can refer to the whole class rather than listing each sound.
  - e.g., "voiceless stops" {p, t, k}; "front vowels"; "back vowels."
- Aspiration in English isn't really a rule "about /p/" — it's a rule about the **natural class of voiceless stops** {p, t, k}, all of which aspirate identically.
- Basic distinctive features (⚠️ gap-fill, useful vocabulary): [±voice], [±nasal], [±continuant] (fricatives/vowels = +, stops = −), [±sonorant] (vowels/nasals/liquids/glides = +, obstruents = −), plus place features like [labial], [coronal], [dorsal].

## Allomorphs (vs. Allophones)
- **Allomorph**: like an allophone, but the alternation is tied to a **specific morpheme** (a meaningful piece of a word), not just any phone. Conditioned phonologically, but the units in alternation carry their own **meaning**, and need not be in complementary distribution overall (they just need to be semantically equivalent forms of the same morpheme).
- **Indefinite article "a/an"**: [ə haʊs] *a house*, [ə pʰɛɹ] *a pear*, [ən aʊɚ] *an hour*, [ən æpəl] *an apple*.
  - [ə] and [ən] are **not** allophones (compare *Krista* [kɹɪstə] vs. *Kristen* [kɹɪstən] — that contrast is about a different morpheme, not evidence against this point, but there's simply no *minimal pair* proving contrast within "a/an" itself since they're the same morpheme). They're **allomorphs**, conditioned by whether the next word starts with a vowel.
  - Adding [n] before a vowel-initial word is an example of ***epenthesis*** — inserting a sound to keep two vowels from clashing.
- **"in-" prefix**: [m], [n], [ŋ] are allomorphs conditioned by nasal place assimilation (see rule 4 above).
- **Past tense "-ed"**: [t]/[d]/[əd] are allomorphs conditioned by voicing assimilation + epenthesis.
- **Plural "-s"**: [s]/[z]/[əz] are allomorphs conditioned by voicing assimilation + epenthesis.

## Syllable structure
A syllable (σ) breaks down as:
- **Syllable**
  - **Onset** (optional) — consonant(s) before the vowel
  - **Rhyme**
    - **Nucleus** (required) — the vowel (or syllabic consonant)
    - **Coda** (optional) — consonant(s) after the vowel

Example: *cat* = Onset /k/, Nucleus /æ/, Coda /t/. *skin* = Onset /sk/, Nucleus /ɪ/, Coda /n/.
- Only the **Nucleus** (typically a vowel) is obligatory; Onset and Coda are optional.
- **Consonant clusters** can appear in onset and/or coda (English allows quite complex ones): e.g. *stop* (CCVC), *plant* (CCVCC), *stray* (CCCV), *splits* (CCCVCC), *scripts* (CCCVCCC).
- The word with the single **most complex syllable structure** among common exam-style examples is often something like ***strengths*** [stɹɛŋkθs/stɹɛŋθs] — onset /str/ (3 C's) + coda /ŋkθs/ (up to 4 C's) around one vowel — versus simpler CCVC words like *stand* or *stuck*. (Note: a word can have *more syllables* — like *onomatopoeia* — without any single syllable being as structurally complex.)

## Phonotactics
- **Phonotactics** = the rules for which sequences of sounds are possible in a given language (e.g., English allows /spr-/ onsets but not /spm-/).
- **Cross-linguistic variation in syllable complexity** (WALS categories):
  - **Simple**: only (C)V syllables allowed.
  - **Moderately complex**: up to CCVC (e.g. *brick*).
  - **Complex**: anything beyond CCVC, e.g. CCVCC (*bricks*).
  - Many languages require much simpler syllables than English allows.
- **English-specific phonotactic facts** (from Yang 2016's study of the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary):
  - Most common English consonants (by word type): **/n/, /l/, /s/, /t/**.
  - Least common: **/ʒ/, /ð/, /θ/** — though /ð/ ironically appears in **"the,"** English's most frequent word *token*.
  - Most common vowels: **/ʌ/, /ə/** (cut, sofa), **/ɪ/** (sit), **/i/** (beet).
  - Least common vowels: **/ɔɪ/** (boy), **/ʊ/** (book), **/aʊ/** (house).
  - Most word types have **2 syllables**; then 3; then 1.
  - Most common syllable shape overall: **onset + no coda** (*so-fa*); next: **onset + a different coda** (*hat*); rare: **no onset, vowel alone** (*a*); rarer still: **no onset, has coda** (*off*); rarest: **onset = coda** (*deed*).

### Type vs. Token (and Stem / Lemma) — ⚠️ Gap-fill
- **Word token**: every individual occurrence of a word, including repeats.
- **Word type**: each *distinct* word form, counted once.
  - "A rose is a rose is a rose" → **3 types** (*a, rose, is*), **8 tokens** (total words counted with repetition).
  - "I came, I saw, I conquered" → **6 tokens**, **4 types** (*I, came, saw, conquered* — "I" repeats 3×).
- **Stem**: the base form of a word before inflectional endings are added (e.g., *walk* is the stem of *walked, walking, walks*).
- **Lemma**: the dictionary citation form representing a whole inflectional paradigm as one entry (e.g., *go* is the lemma covering *go, goes, going, went, gone*).

## Loanword phonology
- When a word is borrowed, it's adapted to fit the **borrowing language's phonemes and phonotactics**.
- **Chinese names into English**: *Xí Jìnpíng* [ɕi tɕinpʰiŋ] → [ʃi dʒɪnpʰɪŋ]; *Hú Jǐntāo* [xu tɕintʰaʊ] → [hu dʒɪntʰaʊ]. English **removes tone** (phonemic in Chinese) and **adds stress** (phonemic in English); substitutes sounds English lacks (no /x/, no /tɕ/).
- **English names into Chinese**: *Joe Biden* → *qiáo bàidēng*; *Trump* → *chuānpǔ* or *tèlǎngpǔ*. Chinese **removes stress, adds tone**; simplifies consonant clusters (no /dʒ/, /tʃɹ/, /mp/ allowed); **no voiced stops** [b, d, g] in Mandarin phonology (only voiceless unaspirated/aspirated pairs like /t/ vs /tʰ/); syllables must end in a vowel or nasal.
- **Loanword adaptation as a general principle**: *all* languages adapt loanwords to their own phonotactics — this is why "correctly" pronouncing a foreign name/word in its original phonology can be socially marked or simply hard for speakers whose language lacks those sounds (e.g., English lacks the French uvular [ʁ] in "Notre Dame," and English's [eɪ] is simply more frequent/natural than [a] in that position). As linguists, we can note this **descriptively** without endorsing the idea that one version is objectively "wrong."
- **Hawaiian phonotactics from place names**: Hawaiian allows only simple (C)V syllables (**no consonant clusters, no codas**), has **no voiced stops**, and clearly permits **nasals** (m, n — visible from place names like *Honolulu, Kamehameha*). Its consonant inventory is small: p, k, ʔ, h, m, n, l, w.

## Worked practice problem: distribution & rule-writing (Greek-style data)
This is exactly the kind of problem the practice exam tests. **General method:**
1. **List every environment** each sound occurs in.
2. **Look for minimal pairs** (same environment, different meaning) → if found, the sounds are **contrastive** (different phonemes).
3. If no minimal pairs exist and the environments never overlap → **complementary distribution** → (if phonetically similar) allophones.
4. **State the rule**: which allophone is conditioned, and by what following/preceding sound? Which one is the elsewhere/default case?

**Example (paralleling your practice exam's Greek data):** Given words like *kano* "do" vs. *xano* "lose," and *kali* "charms" vs. *xali* "plight" — these are **minimal pairs** differing only in [k] vs. [x], in the *same* environment (before a vowel) with *different* meanings → **/k/ and /x/ are contrastive, separate phonemes** in this language.

For a nasal that surfaces as **[n]** before vowels/pauses, **[ɱ]** before a labiodental consonant, and **[ŋ]** before a velar consonant: [n] has the **widest/freest distribution** (appears in the most contexts), while [ɱ] and [ŋ] are restricted to one specific context each → the rule is **nasal place assimilation**: an underlying /n/ **assimilates to the place of articulation of the following consonant**, surfacing as [n] elsewhere.

## Interlinear morpheme glosses — ⚠️ Gap-fill
Linguists present data from other languages in **3 aligned lines**:
1. The word/sentence in the original language (often romanized).
2. A word/morpheme-by-morpheme **gloss** (literal meaning of each piece; grammatical abbreviations like **ASP** = aspect marker, **PST** = past tense, **PL** = plural are in small caps).
3. A natural **free translation** into English.

What you can infer from a gloss like *Mama yiwei wo bing le* / "Mom yiwei I sick ASP" / "Mom is under the impression that I'm sick" (Mandarin) — **this is the exact example on your practice exam**, where the correct answer is **"ALL of these"**:
1. **No syllables end in stop consonants** — assuming a fairly transparent romanization, none of *Mama, yiwei, wo, bing, le* end in [p, t, k].
2. Mandarin has an **aspectual marker** ("le") with **no direct one-word English translation**.
3. Mandarin **does not mark tense** on verbs (*bing* "sick" is untensed).
4. Mandarin **does not mark number/gender agreement** on verbs or adjectives.

## Machine translation & gender (Stanovsky et al. 2019) — ⚠️ Gap-fill (not in the 4 decks, but tested twice on the practice exam)
- Setup: an English sentence containing a noun with no grammatical gender marking (e.g. "the nurse," "the doctor") gets machine-translated into Spanish, which **does** grammatically mark gender.
- **What the Spanish output reveals about Spanish itself:**
  - Spanish **marks gender on nouns** (e.g. *enfermera* fem. vs. *enfermero* masc. for "nurse").
  - Spanish **marks gender on determiners** too (English "the" → Spanish *la* fem. / *el* masc.).
  - Because of this extra agreement marking, the Spanish sentence has a **higher morpheme-to-word ratio** than the English one.
- **Why Stanovsky et al. (2019) cared**: since the source English sentence doesn't specify gender, an MT system doesn't choose randomly — it defaults to **gender-stereotypical** choices (e.g., automatically making "the nurse" feminine, "the doctor" masculine). This reveals **social bias baked into MT training data**: the Spanish output picks stereotypical genders for nouns that were never marked for gender in the English input.

## Broad vs. Narrow Transcription
- **Narrow (phonetic) transcription**: as much phonetic detail/allophonic precision as possible — e.g., marking aspiration [pʰ], flaps [ɾ], etc.
- **Broad (phonemic) transcription**: transcribes only the **phonemes**, leaving the reader to fill in allophonic detail using their own phonological knowledge of the language.
- The same symbols in **[brackets]** for a language you don't know (e.g., raw Finnish data) don't automatically tell you exact pronunciation — without more information about that language's phonology, you can't assume a one-to-one mapping to English-like sounds.
