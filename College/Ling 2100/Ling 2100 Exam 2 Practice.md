# Unit 5 — Morphology

## Parts of speech

**5.1** Which of these is a **function** word?
- A. every
- B. quickly
- C. sandwich
- D. beautiful

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *Every* is a determiner (a quantifier): closed class, hard to paraphrase. The others are content words (noun, adverb, adjective).

**5.2** Which word class is **most likely to gain a brand-new member** this year?
- A. determiners
- B. prepositions
- C. nouns
- D. pronouns

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Nouns are **open class**: new ones are coined all the time. The others are closed class (new members are rare, like conjunction *slash*).

**5.3** *\*Him saw her.* What makes this ungrammatical?
- A. tense
- B. adjective order
- C. number agreement
- D. case on the subject

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** The subject needs **nominative** case (*he*), not accusative (*him*). Same as the slide's *\*Her saw it*. English shows case only on pronouns.

**5.4** Which English word is inflected for case? *(practice exam)*
- A. running
- B. me
- C. cats
- D. the cat

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Me* is the accusative form of *I*. Only pronouns show case in English; *cats* shows number, *running* is a verb form.

**5.5** Which sentence contains a **ditransitive** verb?
- A. She sent him a letter.
- B. The window broke.
- C. I threw the ball.
- D. They laughed.

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *Send* takes 3 arguments: sender (*she*), recipient (*him*), thing sent (*a letter*). B and D are intransitive (1 argument); C is transitive (2).

**5.6** Which word is a counterexample to "verbs describe change over time"?
- A. break
- B. throw
- C. own
- D. melt

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** *Own* describes a **state**, not a change (like the slide's *I love movies*). The others all describe something changing.

**5.7** Which is a counterexample to "nouns describe time-stable entities"? *(practice exam)*
- A. hammer
- B. cat
- C. spinach
- D. explosion

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** An explosion is a brief **event**. The others are stable things. (The practice exam used *earthquake*.)

## Inflection and derivation

**5.8** The *-er* in ***teacher*** is...
- A. bound, inflectional
- B. bound, derivational
- C. free, inflectional
- D. free, derivational

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** It turns the verb *teach* into a **noun**: a POS change, so derivational. It can't stand alone, so bound.

**5.9** The *-er* in ***taller*** is...
- A. bound, inflectional
- B. bound, derivational
- C. free, inflectional
- D. free, derivational

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Trap! Same spelling as 5.8, different morpheme. Comparative *-er* keeps *tall* an adjective, so it's **inflectional**.

**5.10** The *-es* in *cranberries* is... *(practice exam)*
- A. free, inflectional
- B. bound, derivational
- C. bound, inflectional
- D. free, derivational

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Plural suffix: the word stays a noun.

**5.11** Which pair shows **ablaut**?
- A. good / better
- B. go / went
- C. cat / cats
- D. foot / feet

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** The vowel changes, with no affix added (like *man/men*). A and B are suppletion; C is concatenative.

**5.12** Which pair shows **suppletion**?
- A. sing / sang
- B. go / went
- C. walk / walked
- D. sheep / sheep

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Went* replaces the whole root. A is ablaut, C is concatenative, D is no change at all.

**5.13** Why do irregular forms like *be/am/was* survive in English?
- A. they are very frequent, so speakers hear them often enough to preserve them
- B. they are borrowed from French
- C. they are function words
- D. they are suppletive, and suppletion never changes

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Irregulars cluster on **frequent** words. Rare irregulars get regularized: *help*'s old past *holp* became *helped*.

**5.14** In *I will **box** the supplies*, *box* is...
- A. a noun inflected for tense
- B. a compound
- C. a verb made from a noun with no affix
- D. a clipping of *boxing*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** The part of speech changes (noun → verb) without any affix. Same with *a guitar **pick*** (verb → noun).

**5.15** In English, the morpheme *-ness* is... *(practice exam)*
- A. inflectional, bound
- B. inflectional, free
- C. derivational, free
- D. derivational, bound

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** It turns adjectives into nouns (*silly → silliness*).

## Morphemes and word structure

**5.16** Which word contains a **cranberry morpheme**?
- A. blueberry
- B. raspberry
- C. blackberry
- D. understand

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Rasp-* never occurs on its own; it only exists in this one word. *Blue* and *black* are free words; *understand* is two free morphemes.

**5.17** *Enlighten* is an example of a...
- A. circumfix
- B. suffix
- C. prefix
- D. infix

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *En-* and *-en* attach on both sides of *light* at once.

**5.18** In *nationalizes*, which morpheme is **inflectional**?
- A. *nation*
- B. *-al*
- C. *-s*
- D. *-ize*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** *-s* (3rd-person singular) is outermost and doesn't change the POS. *-al* and *-ize* are derivational (N → Adj → V).

**5.19** Why is *\*nationation* impossible?
- A. *nation* is a bound morpheme
- B. it's a blend
- C. English has no suffixes after *-ion*
- D. *-ation* only attaches to verbs, and *nation* is a noun

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Affixes attach to specific categories. *-ation* needs a verb (*nationalize → nationalization*).

**5.20** *Unlockable* meaning "**impossible to lock**" has which structure?
- A. `[[un-lock] -able]`
- B. `[un- [lock-able]]`
- C. `[un- lock -able]` (flat)
- D. `[[un-able] lock]`

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** First *lock + -able* = "lockable" (Adj), then *un-* negates the adjective. A means "able to be unlocked."

**5.21** You want to describe something that **can easily catch fire**. Which structure of *inflammable* is that? *(practice exam)*
- A. `[[in-flame] -able]`
- B. `[in- [flamm-able]]`
- C. `[in- flame -able]` (flat)
- D. `[[in-able] flame]`

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *In- + flame* forms the verb *inflame* first, then *-able*. B means "not flammable," which is why English switched to *flammable*.

## New words

**5.22** Which is a **blend**?
- A. NASA
- B. gym
- C. brunch
- D. desk chair

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** **br**eakfast + l**unch**: parts of 2 words. B is clipping, A is an acronym, D is a compound.

**5.23** *Enthuse* (from *enthusiasm*) is an example of...
- A. clipping
- B. derivation
- C. borrowing
- D. back-formation

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** It makes a new "base" by stripping what *looks like* an affix.

**5.24** Using *Xerox* to mean "photocopy" is...
- A. a word from a person's name
- B. genericization
- C. an acronym
- D. a blend

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** The brand name became the general word (like *Kleenex, Uber*).

**5.25** ⚠️ Match each word to its process: *covidiot*, *vax*, *doomscroll*, *unserious*.

> [!success]- Answer
> - *covidiot* = **blend** (covid + idiot)
> - *vax* = **clipping** (vaccine)
> - *doomscroll* = **compound** (doom + scroll)
> - *unserious* = **derivation** (*un-* + serious)

**5.26** ⚠️ Which word was formed by **clipping**? *(practice exam)*
- A. rizz
- B. 6-7
- C. yassify
- D. doomscroll

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *Rizz* is cut from cha**ris**ma. C is derivation (*-ify*), D is a compound, B isn't word formation.

## Word frequency and typology

**5.27** "A handful of words are extremely frequent; most words are rare." This describes...
- A. Zipf's Brevity Law
- B. a normal distribution
- C. Zipf's Frequency Law (a power law)
- D. a biased corpus

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Few very frequent function words, a long tail of rare content words.

**5.28** "*The* and *of* are short; *oversimplification* is long and rare." This illustrates...
- A. Zipf's Frequency Law
- B. polysemy
- C. a bell curve
- D. Zipf's Brevity Law

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Frequent words are shorter: language is efficient.

**5.29** The most common length of an English word **type** is about 7 characters. If you counted **tokens** (every occurrence) instead, the typical length would be...
- A. longer
- B. shorter
- C. the same
- D. impossible to measure

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** Tokens repeat the frequent words (*the, of, a*), which are short.

**5.30** *Mangtegha-ghlla-ngllagh-yug-tu-kut* ("We want to make a big house") is one word. The language (Yupik) is...
- A. polysynthetic
- B. agglutinating
- C. isolating
- D. analytic

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** The whole sentence is packed into one word.

**5.31** In French *était*, the ending *-ait* marks 3rd person, singular, and imperfective all at once. French is...
- A. isolating
- B. agglutinating
- C. fusional
- D. polysynthetic

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Several meanings fused into one affix, with no separate piece for each. (Agglutinating would give each meaning its own affix.)

**5.32** Turkish *ev-imiz-de* = house-our-in "in our house." This shows Turkish is... *(practice exam)*
- A. polysynthetic
- B. isolating
- C. fusional
- D. agglutinating

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Root + a string of affixes, each with exactly one meaning.

# Unit 6 — Syntax

## Syntax vs. semantics

**6.1** *Colorless green ideas sleep furiously* is...
- A. grammatical and meaningful
- B. grammatical but meaningless
- C. ungrammatical but meaningful
- D. ungrammatical and meaningless

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** Chomsky's example that syntax and semantics are independent.

**6.2** *Me want cookie* is...
- A. ungrammatical, but somewhat meaningful
- B. ungrammatical and meaningless
- C. grammatical and meaningful
- D. grammatical but meaningless

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Wrong case (*me* as subject) and no agreement, but you know what it means. (The practice exam used *Me eat cookie*.)

## English syntax facts

**6.3** *Did the student read the book?* The word *did* shows...
- A. object fronting
- B. a long-distance dependency
- C. do-support and inversion
- D. a relative clause

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** *Do* appears because there's no other auxiliary (do-support), and it goes to the front (inversion).

**6.4** *The paper, she didn't read.* This word order is...
- A. the normal declarative order
- B. ungrammatical
- C. a wh-question
- D. object fronting, used in contrastive contexts

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Fronting the object is allowed only when contrasting (*The book, she read. The paper, she didn't.*).

**6.5** Which sentence contains a **long-distance dependency**?
- A. The student read the book.
- B. What did the student read?
- C. The red ball bounced.
- D. She laughed.

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *What* is at the front but is the object of *read* (*read ___*).

**6.6** ⚠️ *The **dogs** in the yard **bark**.* Which two words stand in a long-distance dependency?
- A. *dogs; bark*
- B. *the; dogs*
- C. *yard; bark*
- D. *in; yard*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** The verb agrees with the head noun *dogs*, skipping over *in the yard*. (The practice exam's version: *The key to the cabinets is...* → *key; is*.)

## Constituents and trees

**6.7** In *The athlete tossed the ball*, which is a constituent?
- A. *athlete tossed*
- B. *tossed the*
- C. *tossed the ball*
- D. *the athlete tossed*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** It answers "What did the athlete do?" and can be conjoined (*tossed the ball and ran*).

**6.8** *What did she throw? — The red ball.* This shows *the red ball* is a constituent using which test?
- A. it can be inflected
- B. it can be conjoined
- C. it can be reordered
- D. it can answer a question

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** The question-answer test.

**6.9** Spoken language comes out one word after another... *(practice exam)*
- A. and so it's stored linearly, with no other structure
- B. but it's mentally organized with hierarchical structure
- C. but it's written with hierarchical structure
- D. but it's processed in a single instant

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** Constituents and trees.

**6.10** Which is the correct structure for *The cat chased a mouse*?
- A. [S [NP the cat] [VP chased [NP a mouse]]]
- B. [S [NP the cat] [VP chased a] [NP mouse]]
- C. [S [S the cat chased] [NP a mouse]]
- D. [S the cat chased a mouse] (flat)

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Every real constituent (*the cat*, *a mouse*, *chased a mouse*) gets its own node. B splits *a* from *mouse*; C has no VP; D has no constituents.

## Phrase structure rules

**6.11** Which pair of rules shows **recursion**?
- A. S → NP VP; NP → Det N
- B. VP → V NP; VP → V
- C. NP → NP PP; PP → P NP
- D. Det → the; N → cat

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** NP contains PP, which contains another NP, and so on forever (*the book of the student in the forest...*).

**6.12** Which pair of rules shows the **one-to-many** property?
- A. N → cat; V → run
- B. NP → NP PP; PP → P NP
- C. S → NP VP; VP → V
- D. NP → Det N; NP → Pronoun

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** The **same** left side (NP) has several possible right sides.

**6.13** Phrase structure rules must be language-specific because...
- A. they are cancelable
- B. they capture word order, which differs across languages
- C. they're only used in NLP
- D. every language has the same lexicon

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** E.g. English puts adjectives before nouns; French mostly after.

**6.14** Using the lecture's grammar fragment (S → NP VP; NP → Pronoun; VP → IntransV; VP → TransV NP; IntransV → *dance*; TransV → *throw*), which sentence can it generate?
- A. *They dance.*
- B. *Dance they.*
- C. *They throw.*
- D. *Throw.*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** S → NP (Pronoun *they*) + VP (IntransV *dance*). B has the wrong order; C needs an object NP for transitive *throw*; D has no subject NP.

**6.15** In a phrase structure tree, the **terminal** nodes are...
- A. the category labels like NP and VP
- B. the S node
- C. the actual words, supplied by the lexicon
- D. the arrows between words

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Non-terminal nodes are the category labels built by the rules.

## Dependency grammar

**6.16** Dependency relations are...
- A. language-specific and generative
- B. recursive and one-to-many
- C. cancelable and calculable
- D. binary, asymmetric, and typed

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** 2 words, a one-way arrow from head to dependent, and a labeled relation type (NSUBJ, OBJ...). B describes PS rules; C describes implicatures.

**6.17** In a dependency graph, the **root** of a sentence is usually...
- A. the first word
- B. the main verb
- C. the subject noun
- D. the determiner

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** The verb determines how many nouns there are and describes the main event.

**6.18** What's the main advantage of dependency grammar for cross-linguistic work?
- A. it works for any word order, including free word order like Latin
- B. it captures grammaticality judgments
- C. it needs movement rules
- D. it is based on phrases

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** That's why NLP uses it (Universal Dependencies). B (capturing grammaticality judgments) is actually its weakness.

**6.19** Which version is more common in real corpora, and why?
- *John threw out the old trash sitting in the kitchen.*
- *John threw the old trash sitting in the kitchen out.*

> [!success]- Answer
> **The first.** Its total dependency length is shorter (14 vs. 20): *out* sits right next to *threw*. Across 37 languages, shorter dependencies are more common than chance (language is efficient).

**6.20** Which is widely used in NLP? *(practice exam)*
- A. morphological reanalysis
- B. phrase structure trees
- C. dependency parsing
- D. Gricean maxims

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.**

## Ambiguity and fieldwork

**6.21** *Hospital sued by 7 foot doctors* is ambiguous because of...
- A. lexical ambiguity
- B. presupposition
- C. underspecified event participation
- D. structural ambiguity

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** [7-foot] doctors (tall) vs. 7 [foot doctors] (podiatrists): two structures.

**6.22** *Iraqi Head Seeks Arms* is ambiguous because of...
- A. structural ambiguity
- B. lexical ambiguity
- C. underspecified event participation
- D. deixis

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Head* (leader / body part) and *arms* (weapons / limbs) each have two senses.

**6.23** *Kids make nutritious snacks* is ambiguous because...
- A. one structure and one sense per word, but kids can participate in "making" in two ways (preparing vs. being the snack)
- B. there are two tree structures
- C. *kids* has two unrelated meanings
- D. it's ungrammatical

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.**

**6.24** In Mandarin, *Sàmǔ chī le shénme?* ("What did Sam eat?") shows that the wh-word...
- A. moves to the front
- B. needs do-support
- C. stays in object position
- D. comes before the verb

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** *Shénme* "what" stays where the object goes, unlike English (and French), which front it.

**6.25** Which is a concern the lecture raised about syntactic fieldwork ethics?
- A. fieldwork should only use English
- B. only written data counts
- C. speakers should never see the analysis
- D. researchers should "give back" to communities (dictionaries, archives, materials), not just publish paywalled articles

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.**

# Unit 7 — Semantics

## Meaning and word relations

**7.1** *Alice has a cat* → *Alice has a pet*. This relationship is...
- A. contradiction
- B. entailment
- C. implicature
- D. presupposition

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** If the first is true, the second must be true. It can't be cancelled (*\*Alice has a cat but no pet*).

**7.2** *Bank* (river) and *bank* (money) are...
- A. accidental homonyms
- B. systematically polysemous
- C. synonyms
- D. antonyms

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Unrelated meanings that happen to share a form; you wouldn't expect the same pair in other languages.

**7.3** *The book is heavy* / *The book is interesting* shows...
- A. accidental homonymy
- B. metaphor
- C. systematic polysemy (object vs. content)
- D. vagueness

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Related senses; the same object/content pattern shows up in other languages.

**7.4** ⚠️ *Chicken* means both a living bird and a type of meat. This is...
- A. accidental homonymy
- B. synonymy
- C. a blend
- D. systematic polysemy

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Same pattern as the practice exam's *lamb*: animal/meat recurs across words and languages.

**7.5** ⚠️ Which pair is **accidental** homonymy? *(practice exam)*
- A. *dinner* (food) / *dinner* (event)
- B. *for* / *four*
- C. *clear* (visually) / *clear* (conceptually)
- D. *apple* (count) / *apple* (mass)

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** Unrelated meanings. A is polysemy; C is metaphor-based polysemy; D is the mass/count distinction.

**7.6** Which question **assumes** the person is short?
- A. *How short is she?*
- B. *How tall is she?*
- C. both
- D. neither

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *Short* is the **marked** antonym. *Tall* is unmarked (neutral).

## Who decides meaning

**7.7** For which word do we most clearly **defer to experts**?
- A. *sandwich*
- B. *soup*
- C. *quark*
- D. *chair*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Physicists define it. (The practice exam used *carbohydrate*.)

**7.8** Wittgenstein argued that the members of the category *game* share...
- A. one defining feature
- B. nothing at all
- C. a genetic essence
- D. family resemblances, with no single feature common to all

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** So word boundaries can be fuzzy.

## Nouns, verbs, adjectives

**7.9** Which is a **relational** noun?
- A. *sheep*
- B. *mother*
- C. *table*
- D. *water*

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** You're always someone's mother: *the mother of my friend* ✓ vs. *?the sheep of my friend*.

**7.10** Which is a **mass** noun?
- A. *mail*
- B. *letters*
- C. *books*
- D. *chairs*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *So much mail* (mass) vs. *so many letters* (count): same stuff, different grammar.

**7.11** *She started the novel* most likely means she started reading or writing it, because *novel* is...
- A. a natural kind
- B. a mass noun
- C. an artifact, so we infer its conventional use
- D. a relational noun

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Compare *?She started the bear*: a natural kind has no conventional activity.

**7.12** Which sentence is **telic**?
- A. *She pushed a cart for an hour.*
- B. *She loved movies.*
- C. *She watched TV.*
- D. *She ran a mile in ten minutes.*

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** There's a built-in endpoint (the mile), and it takes "in X time." A and C are atelic; B is stative.

**7.13** *The glass broke* is fine, but *?The glass hit* is not. This is because *break* is...
- A. stative
- B. causative (it entails the object changes state)
- C. atelic
- D. agentive

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Hit* doesn't entail any change in the thing hit.

**7.14** Which sentence gives the clearest **faultless disagreement**?
- A. *This soup is tasty.*
- B. *This soup is hot.*
- C. *This soup has carrots.*
- D. *This soup is a liquid.*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** *Tasty* is subjective: if you say "no it's not," neither of you is wrong. (The practice exam used *Roller coasters are fun*.)

**7.15** Which adjective order sounds most natural?
- A. *a wooden lovely old table*
- B. *an old wooden lovely table*
- C. *a lovely old wooden table*
- D. *a wooden old lovely table*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** The most subjective adjective (*lovely*) goes furthest from the noun; the most objective (*wooden*) goes closest (Scontras et al.).

**7.16** *Counterfeit money* is not money. *Counterfeit* is...
- A. intersective
- B. subsective
- C. deictic
- D. privative

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Like *fake* and *former*.

**7.17** Which adjective is most clearly **intersective**?
- A. *skillful* (pianist)
- B. *vegetarian* (surgeon)
- C. *former* (president)
- D. *alleged* (thief)

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** Someone who's a vegetarian surgeon is a vegetarian **and** a surgeon. A is subsective (skillful *as* a pianist); C is privative; D isn't necessarily a thief.

## Other word types and metaphor

**7.18** Which word is **deictic**?
- A. *yesterday*
- B. *beautiful*
- C. *table*
- D. *run*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Which day *yesterday* means depends on when it's said.

**7.19** *I missed the damn bus* vs. *I missed the bus*. *Damn* changes...
- A. the truth conditions
- B. the presupposition
- C. only the speaker's expressed attitude
- D. the tense

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** It's an **expressive**: both sentences are true in exactly the same situations.

**7.20** *She shot down every point I made* reflects which conceptual metaphor?
- A. TIME IS MONEY
- B. GOOD IS UP
- C. UNDERSTANDING IS VISION
- D. ARGUMENTS ARE WARS

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.**

**7.21** *I need to budget my time better* reflects...
- A. TIME IS SPACE
- B. TIME IS MONEY
- C. PROJECTS ARE BUILDINGS
- D. GOOD IS UP

> [!success]- Answer

- C. allegory

**7.23** According to Lakoff & Johnson, metaphors are...

# Unit 8 — Pragmatics

## Speech acts and Common Ground

**8.1** Which is a **performative** utterance?
- A. *The sky is blue.*
- B. *She resigned yesterday.*
- C. *I hereby resign.*
- D. *Did you resign?*

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** Saying it (by the right person) makes it true. *Hereby* is the signature word.

**8.2** A random stranger says *You're fired!* to you. Why doesn't it work?
- A. it has no presupposition
- B. it's not a declarative
- C. it violates the maxim of Manner
- D. the speaker lacks the authority; performatives need the right person and context

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.**

**8.3** The Common Ground is...
- A. all true facts in the world
- B. the information speakers mutually take for granted
- C. all the presuppositions in a conversation
- D. everything entailed by what's been said

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.**

**8.4** A: *The quiz is Friday.* B: *No, it's Thursday.* What happens to the Common Ground?
- A. nothing is added until they resolve the disagreement
- B. "The quiz is Friday" is added anyway
- C. both claims are added
- D. the Common Ground is erased

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Assertions are only **proposals** to update the Common Ground; they can be rejected.

## Presupposition

**8.5** *Sam stopped running marathons* presupposes...
- A. Sam runs marathons now
- B. Sam never ran marathons
- C. Sam used to run marathons
- D. Sam likes marathons

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** *Stop* triggers it, and it survives in *Sam didn't stop running marathons* and *Did Sam stop...?*

**8.6** Which inference survives under negation?
- A. entailment
- B. none
- C. implicature
- D. presupposition

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** That's projection.

**8.7** ⚠️ *Lee regrets lying.* → *Lee lied.* Negate it: *Lee doesn't regret lying.* Lee still lied. This inference is...
- A. an entailment
- B. a presupposition
- C. an implicature
- D. a metaphor

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** *Regret* is a **factive** verb, like *know* (*Sam knows it's raining*).

**8.8** Why is *The King of France is bald* a problem?
- A. it presupposes something false, so it's nonsensical rather than just false
- B. it's false
- C. it's ungrammatical
- D. it violates the maxim of Quality

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** Replying "No he's not" misses the problem; you have to say "Wait, there is no King of France!"

## Grice and implicature

**8.9** Which is **not** one of Grice's maxims?
- A. Quantity
- B. Relevance
- C. Politeness
- D. Manner

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** The four are Quality, Quantity, Manner, Relevance.

**8.10** Someone asks the time, and you explain the history of clockmaking. Which maxim did you violate?
- A. Quality
- B. Truth
- C. Politeness
- D. Quantity

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** Too much information. (Arguably Relevance too.)

**8.11** *Some of the cookies are gone.* What does this implicate?
- A. all the cookies are gone
- B. not all the cookies are gone
- C. there were cookies
- D. the speaker ate them

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** It's a scalar implicature: if *all* were true, the speaker would have said so. (C is a presupposition.)

**8.12** Which continuation shows that 8.11's inference is an **implicature**?
- A. *...in fact, all of them are.*
- B. *...but there were no cookies.*
- C. *...and some is a word.*
- D. *...said nobody.*

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** You can cancel it without contradiction.

**8.13** *It's freezing in here* (said to someone next to an open window) → "please close the window." This is...
- A. an entailment
- B. a presupposition
- C. an implicature (via Relevance)
- D. a performative

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** It's cancelable: *It's freezing, but leave it open, I like it.*

**8.14** *I ran* → *I moved*. This inference is...
- A. a metaphor
- B. an implicature
- C. a presupposition
- D. an entailment

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.** It can't be cancelled (*\*I ran but didn't move*) and it disappears under negation (*I didn't run* doesn't mean *I moved*).

**8.15** Which is **not** a property of conversational implicatures? ⚠️
- A. cancelable
- B. projects through negation
- C. calculable
- D. reinforceable

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** That's presuppositions.

**8.16** A recommendation letter for a math PhD says only: *She is always punctual and has neat handwriting.* What's implicated?
- A. she's not a strong mathematician
- B. she's an excellent mathematician
- C. she's late sometimes
- D. nothing

> [!success]- Answer
> **A.** The writer says nothing relevant about math (flouting Quantity and Relevance), like the lecture's philosopher letter.

**8.17** Tickets cost **$9**. B says *I have $9.* Do we infer B has *only* $9?
- A. yes, by Quality
- B. yes, scalar implicature
- C. no: having more is irrelevant to the Question Under Discussion ("Can you afford it?")
- D. no, because it's a presupposition

> [!success]- Answer
> **C.** If tickets cost $10, then *I have $9* would answer "no," and we'd infer B has only $9.

**8.18** What point do Grice, Levinson (the Rembrandt sketch), and Searle (the hamburger) all make?
- A. meaning is the set of true answers
- B. performatives make themselves true
- C. metaphors are fundamental
- D. we infer a lot beyond what's strictly said

> [!success]- Answer
> **D.**

**8.19** In the *Bronston* case, the answer *The company had an account in Zurich* was...
- A. a lie, so it was perjury
- B. literally true but misleading (via implicature), so it wasn't perjury
- C. a presupposition failure
- D. a performative

> [!success]- Answer
> **B.** The law tracks what's **said**, not what's implicated.

**8.20** *Want to grab lunch? — Well, I have a meeting at noon.* The word *well* signals...

# Mixed and Short-Answer

**M.1** For each inference, say whether it's an **entailment**, **presupposition**, or **implicature**. Use the two tests: can it be cancelled? Does it survive negation?
1. *My brother is a surgeon* → *I have a brother*
2. *Mia ate all the pie* → *Mia ate some of the pie*
3. *Can you pass the salt?* → *Please pass the salt*
4. *Jo realized she was late* → *Jo was late*

> [!success]- Answer
> 1. **Presupposition**: *My brother isn't a surgeon* still means you have a brother.
> 2. **Entailment**: can't be cancelled, and *Mia didn't eat all the pie* doesn't mean she ate some.
> 3. **Implicature**: cancelable (*Can you? I'm just curious if your arm works*).
> 4. **Presupposition**: *realize* is factive; *Jo didn't realize she was late* still means she was late.

**M.2** Break *unbelievably* into morphemes. Label each as free/bound and inflectional/derivational, and give the part of speech after each step.

> [!success]- Answer
> *believe* (free root, V) → *believ**able*** (bound, derivational: V → Adj) → ***un**believable* (bound, derivational: still Adj, now negated) → *unbelievab**ly*** (bound, derivational: Adj → Adv). No inflectional morphemes.

**M.3** Using the lecture's grammar (S → NP VP; NP → (Det) (Adj\*) N; NP → NP PP; PP → Prep NP; VP → TransV NP; VP → VP PP), give the bracket structure for *The student read the book in the forest*. Treat *read* as a TransV.

> [!success]- Answer
> [S [NP [Det the] [N student]] [VP [VP [TransV read] [NP [Det the] [N book]]] [PP [Prep in] [NP [Det the] [N forest]]]]]
>
> Bonus: it's ambiguous. With NP → NP PP, *the book in the forest* can be one NP instead (which book? the one in the forest).

**M.5** Name three ways the course showed that "language is efficient."
